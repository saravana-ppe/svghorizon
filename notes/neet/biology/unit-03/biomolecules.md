## 1. Biomolecules – Chemical Composition of Living Tissues

### 1.1 Elemental composition
- All elements in the earth's crust are also present in living tissue. But the **relative abundance of carbon and hydrogen** is **higher in living organisms**.

| Element | % weight in earth's crust | % weight in human body |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen (H) | 0.14 | 9.5 |
| Carbon (C) | 0.03 | 18.5 |
| Oxygen (O) | 46.6 | 65.0 |
| Nitrogen (N) | very little | 3.3 |
| Sulphur (S) | 0.03 | 0.3 |
| Sodium (Na) | 2.8 | 0.2 |
| Calcium (Ca) | 3.6 | 1.5 |
| Magnesium (Mg) | 2.1 | 0.1 |
| Silicon (Si) | 27.7 | negligible |

### 1.2 How to analyse chemical composition
- Grind living tissue in **trichloroacetic acid (Cl<sub>3</sub>CCOOH)** → slurry → strain through cheesecloth → two fractions:
  - **Filtrate = acid-soluble pool** – thousands of organic compounds; molecular weights **18 to about 800 Da** (micromolecules). Roughly represents **cytoplasmic composition**.
  - **Retentate = acid-insoluble fraction** – only **four types**: **proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides and lipids**.
- All carbon compounds from living tissues = **biomolecules**.
- **Inorganic constituents** – determined by burning dried tissue; remaining **ash** contains inorganic elements (Ca, Mg, etc.). Sulphate, phosphate also seen in acid-soluble fraction. Examples: Na<sup>+</sup>, K<sup>+</sup>, Ca<sup>++</sup>, Mg<sup>++</sup>, H<sub>2</sub>O, NaCl, CaCO<sub>3</sub>, PO<sub>4</sub><sup>3−</sup>, SO<sub>4</sub><sup>2−</sup>.

### 1.3 Micromolecules vs macromolecules
- **Micromolecules (biomolecules)** – molecular weight **less than 1000 Da**; in acid-soluble pool.
- **Macromolecules (biomacromolecules)** – in acid-insoluble fraction; proteins, nucleic acids and polysaccharides have molecular weights of **10,000 Da and above**; they are **polymers**.
- **Why are lipids in the acid-insoluble fraction?** Lipids are small (up to 800 Da), but they are arranged into **cell membranes**. On grinding, membranes break into **vesicles that are not water-soluble**, so they separate with the acid-insoluble pool. **Lipids are not strictly macromolecules.**
- True macromolecules = **proteins, nucleic acids, polysaccharides**.

### 1.4 Average composition of cells

| Component | % of total cellular mass |
|---|---|
| **Water** | **70–90** (most abundant) |
| Proteins | 10–15 |
| Carbohydrates | 3 |
| Lipids | 2 |
| Nucleic acids | 5–7 |
| Ions | 1 |

## 2. Amino Acids

- Organic compounds with an **amino group** and an **acidic (carboxyl) group** on the **same carbon – the α-carbon**; hence **α-amino acids**.
- They are **substituted methanes** – four substituents: **hydrogen, carboxyl group, amino group and a variable R group**.
- Only **20 types** occur in proteins.

| R group | Amino acid |
|---|---|
| Hydrogen | **Glycine** |
| Methyl | **Alanine** |
| Hydroxy methyl | **Serine** |

- Based on number of amino and carboxyl groups:
  - **Acidic** – glutamic acid
  - **Basic** – lysine
  - **Neutral** – valine
- **Aromatic** amino acids – **tyrosine, phenylalanine, tryptophan**.
- The –NH<sub>2</sub> and –COOH groups are **ionizable**; structure changes with pH. The form carrying both positive and negative charges is the **zwitterionic form**.

## 3. Lipids

- Generally **water-insoluble**.
- **Fatty acid** – carboxyl group attached to an R group (methyl, ethyl or higher –CH<sub>2</sub> groups; 1 to 19 carbons).
  - **Palmitic acid** – **16 carbons** including carboxyl carbon.
  - **Arachidonic acid** – **20 carbons** including carboxyl carbon.
  - **Saturated** – no double bond; **unsaturated** – one or more C=C double bonds.
- **Glycerol** – **trihydroxy propane**.
- Fatty acids esterified with glycerol → **monoglycerides, diglycerides, triglycerides** = fats and oils (based on melting point). **Oils have lower melting point** (e.g., **gingelly oil**) and remain liquid in winter.
- **Phospholipids** – have phosphorus and a phosphorylated organic compound; found in **cell membranes**. Example: **lecithin**.
- **Neural tissues** have lipids with more complex structures.
- **Cholesterol** is shown as a lipid in NCERT figure.

## 4. Nucleotides and Nucleic Acids

- **Nitrogen bases** (heterocyclic rings): **adenine, guanine, cytosine, uracil, thymine**.
  - **Purines** (substituted): **adenine and guanine**
  - **Pyrimidines** (substituted): **cytosine, uracil, thymine**
- **Nucleoside** = nitrogen base + sugar. Examples: **adenosine, guanosine, thymidine, uridine, cytidine**.
- **Nucleotide** = nitrogen base + sugar + phosphate. Examples: **adenylic acid, thymidylic acid, guanylic acid, uridylic acid, cytidylic acid**.
- Sugar: **ribose** (in RNA) or **2'-deoxyribose** (in DNA).
- **DNA and RNA** consist of nucleotides only (**polynucleotides**) and function as **genetic material**.

> **Exam Tip:** Nucleoside = base + sugar (names end in "-osine"/"-idine"). Nucleotide = base + sugar + phosphate (names end in "-ylic acid"). Adenosine is a nucleoside; adenylic acid is a nucleotide.

## 5. Primary and Secondary Metabolites

- **Primary metabolites** – have identifiable functions and known roles in normal physiological processes (amino acids, sugars, lipids, nucleotides). Found in animal tissues.
- **Secondary metabolites** – found in **plant, fungal and microbial cells**; roles in host not fully understood, but many are useful to human welfare; some have ecological importance.

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| Pigments | Carotenoids, anthocyanins |
| Alkaloids | **Morphine, codeine** |
| Terpenoids | Monoterpenes, diterpenes |
| Essential oils | Lemon grass oil |
| Toxins | **Abrin, ricin** |
| Lectins | **Concanavalin A** |
| Drugs | **Vinblastin, curcumin** |
| Polymeric substances | Rubber, gums, cellulose |

## 6. Proteins

- **Polypeptides** – linear chains of amino acids linked by **peptide bonds**.
- A protein is a **heteropolymer** (20 types of amino acids), not a homopolymer.
- **Essential amino acids** – must be supplied through **diet** (dietary proteins are the source). **Non-essential** – body can make them.
- Functions: transport nutrients across membrane, fight infections, hormones, enzymes, etc.

| Protein | Function |
|---|---|
| **Collagen** | Intercellular ground substance |
| **Trypsin** | Enzyme |
| **Insulin** | Hormone |
| **Antibody** | Fights infectious agents |
| **Receptor** | Sensory reception (smell, taste, hormone, etc.) |
| **GLUT-4** | Enables **glucose transport into cells** |

- **Collagen** – **most abundant protein in the animal world**.
- **RuBisCO (Ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase-oxygenase)** – **most abundant protein in the whole biosphere**.

### 6.1 Structure of proteins (four levels)

| Level | Description |
|---|---|
| **Primary** | **Sequence of amino acids** (positional information). Left end = first amino acid = **N-terminal**; right end = last amino acid = **C-terminal** |
| **Secondary** | Some portions folded as a **helix** (like a revolving staircase). **Only right-handed helices** are observed in proteins. Other regions folded into other forms (e.g., **β-pleated sheet**) |
| **Tertiary** | Long chain folded upon itself like a **hollow woollen ball**; gives a **3-D view**. **Absolutely necessary for the biological activities** of proteins |
| **Quaternary** | Arrangement of **more than one polypeptide (subunits)** with respect to each other. Example: **adult human haemoglobin** – **4 subunits: 2 α + 2 β** |

## 7. Polysaccharides

- Long chains of sugars (monosaccharides as building blocks).

| Polysaccharide | Monomer | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Cellulose** | **Glucose** | **Homopolymer**; plant cell walls; paper and cotton fibre are cellulosic; **no complex helices → cannot hold I<sub>2</sub>** |
| **Starch** | Glucose | Store of energy in **plants**; forms **helical secondary structures** that hold I<sub>2</sub> → **starch–I<sub>2</sub> is blue** |
| **Glycogen** | Glucose | Storage in **animals**; branched |
| **Inulin** | **Fructose** | Polymer of fructose |
| **Chitin** | Amino-sugars (e.g., glucosamine, N-acetyl galactosamine) | **Complex polysaccharide**; exoskeleton of **arthropods**; mostly homopolymer |

- In a polysaccharide chain (e.g., glycogen), the **right end = reducing end**; **left end = non-reducing end**.

> **PYQ Trend:** "Which polysaccharide gives blue colour with iodine?" (starch). "Inulin is a polymer of?" (fructose). "Most abundant protein in biosphere?" (RuBisCO). "Chitin is found in?" (arthropod exoskeleton, fungal walls).

## 8. Enzymes

### 8.1 General
- **Almost all enzymes are proteins**. Some **nucleic acids** behave like enzymes = **ribozymes**.
- Enzymes have primary, secondary and tertiary structure. In the tertiary structure, crevices or pockets form; one such pocket is the **active site**, into which the substrate fits.
- **Enzymes vs inorganic catalysts:** inorganic catalysts work efficiently at **high temperature and pressure**; enzymes get **damaged at high temperatures (above 40°C)**.
- Enzymes from **thermophilic organisms** (hot vents, sulphur springs) remain stable up to **80°–90°C** – **thermal stability**.

### 8.2 Chemical reactions and rate
- **Physical change** – change in shape or state without breaking bonds.
- **Chemical reaction** – bonds broken and new bonds formed.
- **Rate = δP/δt** (amount of product per unit time). With direction specified, it is **velocity**.
- Rule of thumb: rate **doubles or halves for every 10°C change**.
- Example – **carbonic anhydrase**: CO<sub>2</sub> + H<sub>2</sub>O ⇌ H<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub>
  - Without enzyme: about **200 molecules of H<sub>2</sub>CO<sub>3</sub> per hour**
  - With carbonic anhydrase: about **600,000 molecules per second**
  - Rate accelerated about **10 million times**
- **Metabolic pathway** – multistep reaction with each step catalysed by enzymes. Example: **glucose → 2 pyruvic acid** through **ten** enzyme-catalysed reactions (glycolysis).
  - Skeletal muscle, anaerobic: **lactic acid**
  - Aerobic conditions: **pyruvic acid**
  - Yeast fermentation: **ethanol**

### 8.3 How enzymes work – activation energy
- **Substrate (S)** binds the enzyme at the **active site** → obligatory **ES complex** (transient).
- A new structure of substrate called the **transition state structure** is formed; after bond breaking/making, the product is released.
- **Activation energy** = difference in average energy content of S and the transition state.
- If P is at a lower energy level than S → **exothermic** reaction.
- **Enzymes lower the activation energy** barrier, making S → P easier.

### 8.4 Nature of enzyme action
- E + S ⇌ ES → EP → E + P
- **Catalytic cycle:**
  1. Substrate binds to the active site.
  2. Binding **induces the enzyme to alter its shape**, fitting more tightly around the substrate (induced fit).
  3. Active site breaks chemical bonds of substrate → **enzyme–product complex** forms.
  4. Enzyme releases the products; free enzyme is ready for another substrate.

### 8.5 Factors affecting enzyme activity
- Anything that alters the **tertiary structure**: **temperature, pH, substrate concentration, specific chemicals**.
- **Temperature and pH:** each enzyme has an **optimum temperature and optimum pH**; activity declines above and below.
  - **Low temperature** – preserves enzyme in a **temporarily inactive** state.
  - **High temperature** – **destroys** activity (**denaturation**).
- **Substrate concentration:** velocity rises at first, then reaches a **maximum velocity (V<sub>max</sub>)**, not exceeded by further increase in substrate – because enzyme molecules are **saturated**. **K<sub>m</sub>** is the substrate concentration at which velocity is V<sub>max</sub>/2 (shown in NCERT graph).
- **Inhibition:** a chemical that binds and shuts off enzyme activity = **inhibitor**.
  - **Competitive inhibitor** – closely **resembles the substrate** in structure; competes for the substrate-binding site.
  - Example: **malonate** inhibits **succinic dehydrogenase** (substrate **succinate**).
  - Competitive inhibitors are often used in the **control of bacterial pathogens**.

### 8.6 Classification and nomenclature of enzymes
- **6 classes**, each with **4–13 subclasses**; named by a **four-digit number**.

| Class | Reaction catalysed |
|---|---|
| **1. Oxidoreductases / dehydrogenases** | Oxidoreduction between two substrates: S reduced + S' oxidised → S oxidised + S' reduced |
| **2. Transferases** | Transfer of a group G (**other than hydrogen**) between two substrates: S–G + S' → S + S'–G |
| **3. Hydrolases** | **Hydrolysis** of ester, ether, peptide, glycosidic, C–C, C–halide or P–N bonds |
| **4. Lyases** | Removal of groups by mechanisms **other than hydrolysis**, **leaving double bonds** |
| **5. Isomerases** | Inter-conversion of **optical, geometric or positional isomers** |
| **6. Ligases** | **Linking together of 2 compounds** – joining C–O, C–S, C–N, P–O bonds |

> **Mnemonic:** "**O**h **T**he **H**ungry **L**ion **I**s **L**azy" – Oxidoreductases, Transferases, Hydrolases, Lyases, Isomerases, Ligases (in order 1 to 6).

### 8.7 Co-factors
- **Non-protein constituents** bound to the enzyme to make it catalytically active. Protein part = **apoenzyme**. (Apoenzyme + cofactor = holoenzyme.)

| Cofactor type | Nature | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Prosthetic group** | Organic; **tightly bound** to apoenzyme | **Haem** in **peroxidase and catalase** (break down H<sub>2</sub>O<sub>2</sub> to water and oxygen); haem is part of the active site |
| **Co-enzyme** | Organic; **transient** association, usually during catalysis; serve as cofactors in many different reactions | **NAD and NADP** contain the vitamin **niacin** (many coenzymes contain vitamins) |
| **Metal ion** | Form coordination bonds with side chains at the active site and with the substrate | **Zinc** for the proteolytic enzyme **carboxypeptidase** |

- **Catalytic activity is lost when the cofactor is removed** – shows cofactors are crucial.
