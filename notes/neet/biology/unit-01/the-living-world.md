## 1. What is Living?

> **Note:** The current NCERT Chapter 1 (2026-27 reprint) starts directly with diversity and taxonomy. "What is living?" is still in the NEET 2026 syllabus, so the key points (from the earlier NCERT edition) are given here for completeness.

Living organisms show certain characteristics. Some are *defining* (true of all living things and no non-living thing), others are not.

| Characteristic | Is it a defining feature? | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Growth | No | Living things grow by cell division (increase in mass and number). But non-living things like mountains, sand mounds and crystals also "grow" by accumulation of material on the surface. In living things growth is from inside. |
| Reproduction | No | Many organisms do not reproduce: mules, sterile worker bees, infertile human couples. So reproduction cannot be an all-inclusive defining feature. |
| Metabolism | **Yes** | The sum of all chemical reactions in the body. No non-living object shows metabolism. Metabolic reactions can be shown outside the body in cell-free systems (in vitro), but these are neither living nor non-living – they are living reactions. |
| Consciousness (response to stimuli) | **Yes** | All organisms sense their environment and respond to physical, chemical or biological stimuli. Humans are the only organisms aware of themselves (self-consciousness). |
| Cellular organisation | **Yes** | All living things are made of cells. |

> **Remember:** Metabolism, consciousness and cellular organisation are the defining properties of life. Growth and reproduction are NOT defining features. Patients in coma (brain-dead) on life support are an example discussed with respect to consciousness.

## 2. Biodiversity and the Need for Classification

### 2.1 Biodiversity
- Each different kind of plant, animal or organism represents a **species**.
- The number of species known and described is between **1.7 and 1.8 million**. This number or variety of organisms on Earth is called **biodiversity**.
- New organisms are continuously being identified as we explore new and old areas.

### 2.2 Why do we need to classify and name organisms?
- Local names vary from place to place even within a country, which causes confusion.
- So names must be standardised so that one organism has the same name everywhere. This is **nomenclature**.
- Nomenclature is possible only when the organism is correctly described and we know which organism the name refers to. This is **identification**.
- Since it is impossible to study all organisms one by one, we group them into convenient categories based on easily observable characters. This is **classification**.
- The scientific term for these categories is **taxa** (singular: taxon). Examples: "Plants", "Animals", "Mammals", "Dogs", "Wheat" – all are taxa at different levels.

## 3. Taxonomy and Systematics

### 3.1 Taxonomy
- The process of classifying organisms into different taxa based on characteristics is **taxonomy**.
- Basis of modern taxonomic studies: external and internal structure, structure of cell, development process and ecological information.
- The four basic processes of taxonomy are: **characterisation, identification, classification and nomenclature**.
- The earliest classifications were based on the **uses** of organisms (food, clothing, shelter).

### 3.2 Systematics
- The study of different kinds of organisms, their diversity and the **relationships** among them.
- Word derived from the Latin *systema* – systematic arrangement of organisms.
- Linnaeus used *Systema Naturae* as the title of his publication.
- Scope of systematics was later enlarged to include identification, nomenclature and classification. **Systematics takes into account evolutionary relationships** between organisms.

> **Exam Tip:** Taxonomy = characterisation + identification + classification + nomenclature. Systematics = taxonomy + evolutionary relationships (phylogeny). "Systematics" is the broader term.

## 4. Binomial Nomenclature

- Codes for scientific names:
  - Plants: **ICBN** – International Code for Botanical Nomenclature
  - Animals: **ICZN** – International Code of Zoological Nomenclature
- Scientific names ensure that each organism has **only one name**, and that the name has not been used for any other organism.
- **Binomial nomenclature** was given by **Carolus Linnaeus**. Each name has two parts: the **generic name** and the **specific epithet**.
- Example: Mango – *Mangifera indica*. *Mangifera* = genus, *indica* = specific epithet.

### 4.1 Rules of nomenclature (frequently tested)
1. Biological names are generally in **Latin** and written in **italics**. They are Latinised or derived from Latin irrespective of their origin.
2. The first word is the **genus**; the second is the **specific epithet**.
3. When handwritten, both words are **separately underlined**; when printed, they are in italics.
4. The genus starts with a **capital letter**; the specific epithet starts with a **small letter**.
5. The name of the **author** appears after the specific epithet, in abbreviated form. Example: *Mangifera indica* **Linn.** – means the species was first described by Linnaeus.

> **Common Mistake:** "Mangifera Indica" is WRONG (capital I). "*Mangifera indica*" is correct. When handwritten, underline "Mangifera" and "indica" separately, not as one continuous line.

## 5. Taxonomic Categories and Hierarchy

- Classification is not a single step but a **hierarchy of steps**. Each step represents a **rank or category**.
- Since each category is part of the overall taxonomic arrangement, it is called a **taxonomic category**, and all categories together form the **taxonomic hierarchy**.
- Each category is a **unit of classification** and is called a **taxon**.
- Taxonomic groups are distinct biological entities, not merely morphological aggregates.
- Obligate categories in ascending order: **Species → Genus → Family → Order → Class → Phylum (Division in plants) → Kingdom**.
- **Species is the lowest category** for all organisms.

### 5.1 Each category explained

| Category | Definition (NCERT) | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Species** | Group of individuals with fundamental similarities; distinguished from closely related species by distinct morphological differences | *indica* (mango), *tuberosum* (potato), *leo* (lion), *sapiens* (human) |
| **Genus** | Group of related species with more characters in common than species of other genera; aggregate of closely related species | *Solanum* (potato, brinjal, *S. nigrum*); *Panthera* (lion *P. leo*, leopard *P. pardus*, tiger *P. tigris*); *Felis* (cats) |
| **Family** | Group of related genera with fewer similarities than genus and species; characterised on both vegetative and reproductive features (in plants) | Solanaceae (*Solanum*, *Petunia*, *Datura*); Felidae (*Panthera*, *Felis*); Canidae (dog) |
| **Order** | Assemblage of families showing a few similar characters; identified on aggregates of characters | Polymoniales (Convolvulaceae, Solanaceae – based on floral characters); Carnivora (Felidae, Canidae) |
| **Class** | Includes related orders | Mammalia (orders Primata – monkey, gorilla, gibbon; and Carnivora – tiger, cat, dog) |
| **Phylum / Division** | Classes with common features; "Division" is used for plants | Chordata (fishes, amphibians, reptiles, birds, mammals – all have notochord and dorsal hollow neural system) |
| **Kingdom** | Highest category | Animalia, Plantae |

### 5.2 Trends in the hierarchy
- Going **up** from species to kingdom, the **number of common characteristics decreases**.
- Lower the taxon, **more** characteristics the members share.
- Higher the category, **greater the difficulty** of determining relationships with other taxa at the same level. So classification becomes more complex at higher levels.
- Taxonomists have also developed **sub-categories** (e.g., sub-species, sub-family) for more precise placement.
