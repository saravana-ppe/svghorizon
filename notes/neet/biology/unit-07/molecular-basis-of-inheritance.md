## 1. DNA – Structure

### 1.1 Length of DNA (as base pairs)

| Organism | DNA size |
|---|---|
| Bacteriophage **φ×174** | **5386 nucleotides** |
| Bacteriophage **lambda** | **48,502 bp** |
| ***Escherichia coli*** | **4.6 × 10<sup>6</sup> bp** |
| **Human (haploid)** | **3.3 × 10<sup>9</sup> bp** |

### 1.2 Polynucleotide chain
- Nucleotide = **nitrogenous base + pentose sugar + phosphate**.
  - Sugar: **ribose (RNA)**, **deoxyribose (DNA)**.
  - **Purines:** adenine, guanine. **Pyrimidines:** cytosine, uracil, thymine.
  - **Cytosine** common to both; **thymine** in DNA; **uracil** in RNA in place of thymine.
- **Nucleoside** = base linked to OH of **1' C** of pentose through an **N-glycosidic linkage** (e.g., adenosine/deoxyadenosine).
- **Nucleotide** = phosphate linked to OH of **5' C** of a nucleoside through a **phosphoester linkage**.
- Two nucleotides joined by a **3'–5' phosphodiester linkage** → dinucleotide → polynucleotide.
- **5'-end** has a free phosphate; **3'-end** has a free OH at 3' C.
- **Backbone = sugar and phosphates**; bases project from it.
- In RNA: **additional –OH at 2' position** of ribose; **uracil instead of thymine** (thymine = **5-methyl uracil**).

### 1.3 History
- **Friedrich Meischer (1869)** – first identified DNA as an acidic substance in the nucleus; named it **"Nuclein"**.
- **James Watson and Francis Crick (1953)** – **Double Helix model**, based on **X-ray diffraction data** of **Maurice Wilkins and Rosalind Franklin**.
- **Erwin Chargaff** – in double-stranded DNA, **A/T and G/C ratios are constant and equal one**.

### 1.4 Salient features of the double helix
1. **Two polynucleotide chains**; **sugar–phosphate backbone** outside; **bases project inside**.
2. **Anti-parallel polarity** – one chain 5'→3', the other 3'→5'.
3. Bases paired by **hydrogen bonds**: **A = T (two H-bonds)**; **G ≡ C (three H-bonds)**. A purine always comes opposite a pyrimidine → **uniform distance** between strands.
4. **Right-handed** coiling. **Pitch = 3.4 nm**; about **10 bp per turn**; distance between bp ≈ **0.34 nm**.
5. **Stacking of base pairs** (plane of one over the other) + H-bonds give stability.

- **Complementarity** – if the sequence of one strand is known, the other can be predicted; each strand can act as a **template**.

### 1.5 Central dogma
- Proposed by **Francis Crick**: **DNA → RNA → Protein**.
- In some viruses, information flows from **RNA to DNA** = **reverse transcription**.

> **Exam Tip (Chargaff):** If a dsDNA has 20% cytosine → G = 20%, so G + C = 40%, A + T = 60%, **A = 30%**. (NCERT Exercise Q2.)

## 2. Packaging of DNA Helix

- Length of DNA in a typical mammalian cell = **6.6 × 10<sup>9</sup> bp × 0.34 × 10<sup>−9</sup> m/bp ≈ 2.2 metres**, far greater than the nucleus (about **10<sup>−6</sup> m**).
- ***E. coli*** DNA length **1.36 mm** → about **4 × 10<sup>6</sup> bp**.

### 2.1 Prokaryotes
- No defined nucleus, but DNA is not scattered. **Negatively charged DNA** is held with **positively charged proteins** in a region called **nucleoid**; organised in **large loops**.

### 2.2 Eukaryotes
- **Histones** – positively charged, **basic** proteins; rich in basic amino acids **lysine and arginine** (positive side chains).
- **Histone octamer** – unit of **eight** histone molecules.
- **Nucleosome** – negatively charged DNA wrapped around the positively charged histone octamer; contains about **200 bp** of DNA.
- Nucleosomes = repeating units of **chromatin**; seen as **"beads-on-string"** under the electron microscope.
- Beads-on-string → **chromatin fibres** → coiled and condensed at **metaphase** → **chromosomes**.
- Higher-level packaging requires **Non-Histone Chromosomal (NHC) proteins**.

| Euchromatin | Heterochromatin |
|---|---|
| **Loosely packed**; stains **light** | **Densely packed**; stains **dark** |
| **Transcriptionally active** | **Inactive** |

## 3. The Search for Genetic Material

### 3.1 Griffith's transforming principle (1928)
- Bacterium: ***Streptococcus pneumoniae*** (pneumococcus).
  - **S strain** – smooth shiny colonies; **mucous (polysaccharide) coat**; **virulent** (mice die).
  - **R strain** – rough colonies; no coat; **non-virulent**.

| Injected into mice | Result |
|---|---|
| Live S strain | Mice **die** |
| Live R strain | Mice **live** |
| Heat-killed S strain | Mice **live** |
| **Heat-killed S + live R** | Mice **die**; **living S bacteria recovered** |

- Conclusion: R strain was **transformed** by heat-killed S strain; some **"transforming principle"** (genetic material) was transferred. Biochemical nature not defined.

### 3.2 Avery, MacLeod and McCarty (1933–44)
- Earlier, genetic material was thought to be **protein**.
- Purified biochemicals from heat-killed S cells: **DNA alone** transformed R into S.
- **Proteases and RNases did not affect** transformation; **DNase inhibited** it.
- Concluded **DNA is the hereditary material** – but not all biologists were convinced.

### 3.3 Hershey and Chase (1952) – unequivocal proof
- Worked with **bacteriophages** infecting ***E. coli***.
- Viruses grown on **radioactive phosphorus (<sup>32</sup>P)** → **radioactive DNA** (DNA has P, protein doesn't).
- Viruses grown on **radioactive sulphur (<sup>35</sup>S)** → **radioactive protein** (protein has S, DNA doesn't).
- Phages allowed to infect bacteria → **blending** (removes viral coats) → **centrifugation** (separates virus particles from bacteria).
- **Bacteria infected with <sup>32</sup>P phage were radioactive**; with <sup>35</sup>S phage were **not radioactive**.
- Conclusion: **DNA is the genetic material** passed from virus to bacteria.

## 4. Properties of Genetic Material – DNA vs RNA

- In some viruses **RNA** is the genetic material: **Tobacco Mosaic Virus, QB bacteriophage**.

**Criteria for a genetic material:**
1. Able to **generate its replica** (replication).
2. **Chemically and structurally stable**.
3. Provide scope for **slow changes (mutation)** required for evolution.
4. Able to **express itself** as "Mendelian characters".

| Criterion | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Replication | Yes (complementarity) | Yes |
| Stability | **More stable**: double-stranded, complementary strands reanneal after heating; **no 2'-OH**; **thymine** in place of uracil gives extra stability | **Less stable**: **2'-OH is reactive** → labile, easily degraded; catalytic, hence reactive |
| Mutation | Slower | **Faster** (RNA viruses with short life span mutate and evolve faster) |
| Expression | Depends on RNA for protein synthesis | Can **directly code** for proteins |

- Proteins fail the first criterion (cannot replicate).
- **DNA is better for storage** of genetic information; **RNA is better for transmission**.

## 5. RNA World

- **RNA was the first genetic material.**
- Essential life processes (**metabolism, translation, splicing**) evolved around RNA.
- RNA acted as **genetic material and catalyst** (some reactions are catalysed by RNA, not proteins), but being catalytic it was **reactive and unstable**.
- **DNA evolved from RNA** with chemical modifications for stability; double-stranded DNA with complementary strand resists change and evolved **repair**.

## 6. DNA Replication

### 6.1 Semiconservative model
- Watson and Crick: "*It has not escaped our notice that the specific pairing we have postulated immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material*" (1953).
- The two strands separate and each acts as a **template** → each new DNA has **one parental and one new strand** = **semiconservative replication**.

### 6.2 Meselson and Stahl experiment (1958)
1. ***E. coli*** grown for many generations in **<sup>15</sup>NH<sub>4</sub>Cl** (heavy isotope; **not radioactive**) as the only N source → **heavy (<sup>15</sup>N) DNA**.
2. Cells transferred to **<sup>14</sup>NH<sub>4</sub>Cl** (normal) medium; samples taken at intervals.
3. DNA separated by **CsCl (caesium chloride) density gradient centrifugation**.

| Time after transfer (*E. coli* divides every 20 min) | DNA density |
|---|---|
| 0 min | All heavy (<sup>15</sup>N/<sup>15</sup>N) |
| **20 min (1st generation)** | All **hybrid** (intermediate density) |
| **40 min (2nd generation)** | **Equal amounts of hybrid and light** DNA |
| **80 min (4th generation)** | **1/8 hybrid, 7/8 light** |

- **Taylor and colleagues (1958)** used **radioactive thymidine** on ***Vicia faba*** (faba beans) → proved semiconservative replication in **chromosomes**.

### 6.3 Machinery and enzymes
- Main enzyme: **DNA-dependent DNA polymerase** (uses DNA template; polymerises deoxynucleotides).
- *E. coli* (4.6 × 10<sup>6</sup> bp) replicates in **18 minutes** → average rate about **2000 bp per second**. Human diploid content: **6.6 × 10<sup>9</sup> bp**.
- Polymerases must be **fast and highly accurate** (errors → mutations).
- **Deoxyribonucleoside triphosphates** serve **dual purposes**: act as **substrates** and provide **energy** (two terminal high-energy phosphates).
- **Replication fork** – small opening of the helix where replication occurs (strands cannot be separated over the entire length due to high energy requirement).
- DNA polymerase catalyses polymerisation **only in 5' → 3' direction**:
  - On the template with polarity **3' → 5'** → replication is **continuous** (leading strand).
  - On the template with polarity **5' → 3'** → replication is **discontinuous** (lagging strand); fragments (Okazaki fragments) joined by **DNA ligase**.
- DNA polymerases **cannot initiate** replication on their own.
- Replication starts at a definite region = **origin of replication**. Hence, a DNA piece to be propagated in recombinant DNA work needs a **vector** (provides the origin).
- In eukaryotes, replication occurs in the **S-phase**. Failure of cell division after DNA replication → **polyploidy**.

> **PYQ Trend:** Meselson–Stahl (<sup>15</sup>N not radioactive; CsCl gradient; hybrid after 20 min), Hershey–Chase (<sup>32</sup>P DNA, <sup>35</sup>S protein), Griffith (S and R strains), 5'→3' polymerisation, DNA ligase joins discontinuous fragments.

## 7. Transcription

- Copying genetic information from **one strand of DNA into RNA**.
- Complementarity applies, except **adenine pairs with uracil**.
- Unlike replication (entire DNA), only **a segment of DNA and only one strand** is copied.
- **Why not both strands?** (i) They would code for **two different RNAs/proteins** from the same segment. (ii) The two RNAs would be complementary and form **double-stranded RNA**, preventing translation.

### 7.1 Transcription unit
Three regions: **promoter, structural gene, terminator**.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Template strand** | Strand with polarity **3' → 5'**; acts as template (RNA polymerase also works 5'→3') |
| **Coding strand** | Strand with polarity **5' → 3'**; **same sequence as RNA** (T instead of U); displaced; does not code for anything, but all references are made to it |
| **Promoter** | Located towards the **5'-end (upstream)** of the structural gene (w.r.t. coding strand); **binding site for RNA polymerase**; defines the template and coding strands |
| **Terminator** | Located towards the **3'-end (downstream)** of the coding strand; defines the end of transcription |

- Additional regulatory sequences may be further upstream or downstream of the promoter.

### 7.2 Transcription unit and gene
- **Gene** – functional unit of inheritance; hard to define in terms of DNA sequence.
- **Cistron** – segment of DNA coding for a **polypeptide**.
  - **Monocistronic** – mostly **eukaryotes**
  - **Polycistronic** – mostly **bacteria (prokaryotes)**
- Eukaryotic genes are **split genes**:
  - **Exons** – coding/expressed sequences; **appear in mature RNA**
  - **Introns** – intervening sequences; **do not appear in mature RNA**
- Regulatory sequences are loosely called **regulatory genes**, though they do not code for RNA or protein.

### 7.3 Types of RNA (in bacteria)

| RNA | Role |
|---|---|
| **mRNA** (messenger) | Provides the **template** |
| **tRNA** (transfer) | **Brings amino acids** and **reads the genetic code** |
| **rRNA** (ribosomal) | **Structural and catalytic** role in translation |

### 7.4 Transcription in bacteria
- **A single DNA-dependent RNA polymerase** transcribes **all types of RNA**.
- **Initiation:** RNA polymerase binds to the **promoter**; associates transiently with the **initiation factor (σ, sigma)**.
- **Elongation:** uses **nucleoside triphosphates** as substrate; polymerises following complementarity; opens the helix. Only a short stretch of RNA remains bound to the enzyme.
- **Termination:** at the terminator, associates with **termination factor (ρ, rho)**; nascent RNA and polymerase fall off.
- **RNA polymerase itself catalyses only elongation**; σ and ρ alter its specificity to initiate or terminate.
- In bacteria, mRNA needs **no processing**; transcription and translation occur in the **same compartment** → **coupled transcription and translation** (translation may begin before transcription ends).

### 7.5 Transcription in eukaryotes – two complexities
**(i) Three RNA polymerases in the nucleus:**

| RNA polymerase | Transcribes |
|---|---|
| **RNA polymerase I** | **rRNAs – 28S, 18S, 5.8S** |
| **RNA polymerase II** | **Precursor of mRNA – heterogeneous nuclear RNA (hnRNA)** |
| **RNA polymerase III** | **tRNA, 5S rRNA, snRNAs** (small nuclear RNAs) |

**(ii) Processing of primary transcript (hnRNA):**
- **Splicing** – **introns removed**, **exons joined** in a defined order.
- **Capping** – an unusual nucleotide, **methyl guanosine triphosphate**, added to the **5'-end**.
- **Tailing** – **200–300 adenylate residues** added at the **3'-end** in a **template-independent** manner (poly-A tail).
- Fully processed hnRNA = **mRNA**, transported out of the nucleus for translation.
- Split genes are probably an **ancient feature**; introns are reminiscent of antiquity; splicing represents the dominance of the **RNA world**.

> **Remember – Pol I, II, III:** "**I** – rRNA (big ones), **II** – mRNA (hnRNA), **III** – tRNA, 5S rRNA, snRNA."

## 8. Genetic Code

### 8.1 Deciphering the code
- **George Gamow** (physicist) – 4 bases must code for 20 amino acids → code should be a combination of **three nucleotides**: 4<sup>3</sup> = **64 codons**.
- **Har Gobind Khorana** – chemical method to synthesise **RNA with defined combinations of bases** (homopolymers and copolymers).
- **Marshall Nirenberg** – **cell-free system** for protein synthesis → code deciphered.
- **Severo Ochoa** – enzyme **polynucleotide phosphorylase** – polymerises RNA with defined sequences **template-independently**.
- A **checker-board** for the genetic code was prepared.

### 8.2 Salient features of the genetic code
1. **Triplet codon**. **61 codons code for amino acids**; **3 codons are stop codons**.
2. **Degenerate** – some amino acids coded by **more than one codon**.
3. Read **contiguously**; **no punctuations** (commaless).
4. **Nearly universal** – e.g., **UUU = phenylalanine** from bacteria to humans. Exceptions in **mitochondrial codons** and some **protozoans**.
5. **AUG has dual functions** – codes for **methionine** and acts as the **initiator codon**.
6. **UAA, UAG, UGA** – **stop (terminator) codons**.
- The code is also **unambiguous and specific** – one codon codes for only one amino acid.

> **Exam Tip:** mRNA AUG UUU UUC UUC UUU UUU UUC → Met-Phe-Phe-Phe-Phe-Phe-Phe. Predicting mRNA from amino acid sequence is difficult because the code is **degenerate** (Phe = UUU or UUC).

### 8.3 Mutations and genetic code
- **Point mutation** in β-globin gene → **glutamate to valine** → **sickle-cell anaemia**.
- Insertion/deletion illustrated with "**RAM HAS RED CAP**":
  - Insert one letter: RAM HAS BRE DCA P (reading frame changes)
  - Insert two letters: RAM HAS BIR EDC AP (changes)
  - **Insert three letters: RAM HAS BIG RED CAP** (reading frame restored; one extra "word")
  - Deleting letters similarly shifts the frame; deleting three removes one word.
- **Insertion or deletion of one or two bases** changes the reading frame from that point = **frame-shift mutations**.
- **Insertion or deletion of three (or multiples)** adds/removes one or more codons (amino acids); **reading frame unaltered** beyond that.

## 9. tRNA – The Adapter Molecule

- **Francis Crick** postulated an **adapter molecule** to read the code and bind specific amino acids.
- tRNA was earlier called **sRNA (soluble RNA)**; its adapter role was assigned later.
- Features:
  - **Anticodon loop** – bases **complementary** to the codon.
  - **Amino acid acceptor end** – binds the amino acid.
  - tRNAs are **specific for each amino acid**.
  - **Initiator tRNA** – specific tRNA for initiation.
  - **No tRNAs for stop codons**.
- **Secondary structure** looks like a **clover-leaf**; actual (3-D) structure is a compact **inverted L**.

## 10. Translation

- **Polymerisation of amino acids into a polypeptide**; order defined by the mRNA base sequence. Amino acids joined by **peptide bonds** (energy required).
- **Charging of tRNA (aminoacylation):** amino acids **activated in the presence of ATP** and linked to their **cognate tRNA**.
- **Ribosome** – the cellular factory for protein synthesis; **structural RNAs + about 80 different proteins**. Inactive state: **large and small subunits**.
  - When the **small subunit encounters mRNA**, translation begins.
  - Large subunit has **two sites** for subsequent amino acids to bind close enough for peptide bond formation.
  - Ribosome also acts as a **catalyst**: **23S rRNA in bacteria is the enzyme (ribozyme)** for peptide bond formation.
- **Translational unit** – mRNA sequence flanked by the **start codon (AUG)** and a **stop codon**; codes for a polypeptide.
- **Untranslated regions (UTRs)** – at the **5'-end (before start codon)** and **3'-end (after stop codon)**; required for **efficient translation**.

**Steps:**
1. **Initiation** – ribosome binds mRNA at the **start codon AUG**, recognised only by the **initiator tRNA**.
2. **Elongation** – aminoacyl-tRNA complexes bind sequentially to the codon by complementary pairing with the **anticodon**; ribosome moves codon to codon; amino acids added one by one.
3. **Termination** – a **release factor** binds the **stop codon**, terminating translation and releasing the polypeptide.

## 11. Regulation of Gene Expression

- In eukaryotes, regulation can occur at:
  1. **Transcriptional level** (formation of primary transcript)
  2. **Processing level** (regulation of splicing)
  3. **Transport of mRNA** from nucleus to cytoplasm
  4. **Translational level**
- Genes are expressed according to **metabolic, physiological or environmental conditions** (e.g., **β-galactosidase** made by *E. coli* only when **lactose** is present). Development and differentiation also result from coordinated gene regulation.
- In **prokaryotes**, control of the **rate of transcriptional initiation** is the predominant site.
- Regulatory proteins act **positively (activators)** or **negatively (repressors)**.
- **Operator** – sequence adjacent to the promoter; binds a **repressor**. Each operon has its **specific operator and repressor** (lac operator binds only lac repressor).

## 12. The Lac Operon

- Elucidated by geneticist **François Jacob** and biochemist **Jacques Monod** – first **transcriptionally regulated system**.
- **Operon** – a **polycistronic structural gene** regulated by a **common promoter and regulatory genes**. Common in bacteria: **lac, trp, ara, his, val operons**.

### 12.1 Components

| Gene | Product | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **i gene** (regulatory; "i" from **inhibitor**, not inducer) | **Repressor** | Binds operator; blocks transcription |
| **z gene** | **β-galactosidase (β-gal)** | **Hydrolysis of lactose** into **galactose and glucose** |
| **y gene** | **Permease** | Increases **permeability** of the cell to **β-galactosides** |
| **a gene** | **Transacetylase** | – |

- All three gene products are needed for **lactose metabolism**.

### 12.2 Working
- **Inducer = lactose** (substrate of β-galactosidase; also **allolactose**). Regulates switching on and off.
- **Repressor is synthesised all the time (constitutively)** from the i gene.
- **Absence of lactose:** repressor binds the **operator** → prevents RNA polymerase from transcribing → **operon OFF**.
- **Presence of lactose (and absence of glucose):** lactose enters via **permease** (a very **low level of lac operon expression** must always be present, otherwise lactose cannot enter). Inducer **inactivates the repressor** → RNA polymerase accesses the promoter → **transcription proceeds** → **operon ON**.
- **Regulation of enzyme synthesis by its substrate.**
- **Glucose or galactose cannot act as inducers** for the lac operon.
- The operon **shuts down** some time after lactose is added because lactose is used up (hydrolysed) → repressor becomes active again.
- Regulation by repressor = **negative regulation**. Lac operon is also under **positive regulation** (beyond scope).

> **PYQ Trend:** Functions of z, y, a genes; i gene codes for repressor; lactose/allolactose as inducer; repressor made constitutively; negative regulation. Very frequently asked.

## 13. Human Genome Project (HGP)

### 13.1 Why a mega project
- Launched in **1990**; human genome ≈ **3 × 10<sup>9</sup> bp**; at **US $3 per bp** → about **9 billion US dollars**.
- Storing the sequence in books (1000 letters per page, 1000 pages per book) → **3300 books** for one human cell.
- Needed high-speed computation → development of **Bioinformatics**.

### 13.2 Goals of HGP
1. Identify all the approximately **20,000–25,000 genes** in human DNA.
2. Determine the sequences of the **3 billion chemical base pairs**.
3. **Store** the information in databases.
4. **Improve tools** for data analysis.
5. **Transfer related technologies** to other sectors such as industries.
6. Address the **ethical, legal and social issues (ELSI)**.

- **13-year project** coordinated by the **U.S. Department of Energy and the National Institute of Health**; **Wellcome Trust (U.K.)** became a major partner; contributions from Japan, France, Germany, China. **Completed in 2003**.
- Non-human model organisms sequenced: **bacteria, yeast, *Caenorhabditis elegans*** (free-living non-pathogenic nematode), ***Drosophila***, plants (**rice, *Arabidopsis***).

### 13.3 Methodologies
- **Expressed Sequence Tags (ESTs)** – identifying all genes **expressed as RNA**.
- **Sequence Annotation** – blind approach: sequence the **whole genome** (coding + non-coding), then assign functions.
- DNA isolated → random **fragments** → cloned in hosts (**bacteria and yeast**) using vectors **BAC (bacterial artificial chromosome)** and **YAC (yeast artificial chromosome)**.
- Sequenced with **automated DNA sequencers** based on **Frederick Sanger's method** (Sanger also developed a method for determining amino acid sequences in proteins).
- Sequences arranged using **overlapping regions**; aligned by **computer programs**; annotated and assigned to chromosomes.
- **Chromosome 1** sequence completed last, in **May 2006** (of 24 chromosomes – 22 autosomes + X + Y).
- Genetic and physical maps generated using **polymorphism of restriction endonuclease recognition sites** and **microsatellites**.

### 13.4 Salient features of the human genome
- Contains **3164.7 million bp**.
- Average gene has **3000 bases**; largest known human gene is **dystrophin** at **2.4 million bases**.
- Total genes estimated at **30,000** (much lower than earlier estimates of 80,000–1,40,000).
- **99.9%** of nucleotide bases are **exactly the same in all humans**.
- Functions unknown for **over 50%** of discovered genes.
- **Less than 2%** of the genome **codes for proteins**.
- **Repeated sequences** make up a very large portion; repeated hundreds to thousands of times; no direct coding function but shed light on chromosome structure, dynamics and evolution.
- **Chromosome 1 has the most genes (2968)**; **Y has the fewest (231)**.
- About **1.4 million SNPs** (single nucleotide polymorphisms, "snips") identified.

### 13.5 Applications
- New approach to biological research – study all genes, all transcripts of a tissue/organ/tumour, gene–protein networks.
- Better diagnosis, treatment and prevention of disorders; applications in health care, agriculture, energy, environmental remediation.

## 14. DNA Fingerprinting

- **99.9%** of the base sequence is the same among humans; differences make each individual unique.
- DNA fingerprinting = quick way to compare DNA sequences of two individuals by identifying differences in **repetitive DNA**.

### 14.1 Repetitive and satellite DNA
- **Repetitive DNA** – a small stretch of DNA repeated many times.
- In **density gradient centrifugation**, bulk DNA forms a **major peak**; small peaks = **satellite DNA**.
- Satellite DNA classified (by base composition, length, number of repeats) as **micro-satellites, mini-satellites**, etc.
- **Do not code for proteins**, but form a large portion of the genome; show **high degree of polymorphism** → basis of DNA fingerprinting.
- DNA from **every tissue** (blood, hair follicle, skin, bone, saliva, sperm) of an individual shows the **same polymorphism** → forensic use.
- Polymorphisms are **inherited** → basis of **paternity testing**.

### 14.2 DNA polymorphism
- Arises from **mutations** (in somatic or germ cells). Germ-cell mutations that don't impair reproduction spread in the population.
- **DNA polymorphism** – if **more than one variant (allele)** at a locus occurs in the population with a frequency **greater than 0.01**.
- More likely in **non-coding sequences** (no immediate effect on reproductive ability) → accumulate over generations.

### 14.3 Technique (Alec Jeffreys)
- Developed by **Alec Jeffreys**; used **Variable Number of Tandem Repeats (VNTR)** as a probe – a satellite DNA with very high polymorphism.
- Steps (**Southern blot hybridisation** with radiolabelled VNTR probe):
  1. **Isolation of DNA**
  2. **Digestion** of DNA by **restriction endonucleases**
  3. **Separation** of fragments by **electrophoresis**
  4. **Transferring (blotting)** to synthetic membranes – **nitrocellulose or nylon**
  5. **Hybridisation** using **labelled VNTR probe**
  6. **Detection** by **autoradiography**
- **VNTR** belongs to **mini-satellites**; small sequence arranged **tandemly** in many copies; copy number varies from chromosome to chromosome; size varies **0.1 to 20 kb**.
- Autoradiogram shows many bands of different sizes – **characteristic pattern for each individual**, except **monozygotic (identical) twins**.
- Sensitivity increased by **PCR** → **DNA from a single cell** is enough.
- **Applications:** **forensic science**, **paternity disputes**, determining **population and genetic diversities**, evolutionary biology.
