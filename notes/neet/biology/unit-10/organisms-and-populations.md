## 1. Ecology – Basic Concepts

- **Ecology** is the subject that studies the **interactions among organisms** and **between organisms and their physical (abiotic) environment**.
- Ecology is concerned with **four levels of biological organisation**: **organisms, populations, communities and biomes**.
- Two types of questions can be asked at any level:
  - **"How-type"** questions – seek the **mechanism** behind a process (e.g., how the bulbul sings – voice box and vibrating bone).
  - **"Why-type"** questions – seek the **significance** of a process (e.g., why it sings – to communicate with its mate in the breeding season).
- **Ramdeo Misra** (1908–1998) – revered as the **Father of Ecology in India**; worked at **Banaras Hindu University**; his efforts led to the **National Committee for Environmental Planning and Coordination (1972)** → later the **Ministry of Environment and Forests (1984)**.

## 2. Organism and Its Environment [Beyond current NCERT]

> **Note**
> The 2026-27 NCERT reprint has dropped the section "Organism and its Environment", but the NEET 2026 syllabus still lists "Organisms and environment". The core ideas below were in the earlier NCERT edition and have been asked in NEET PYQs.

### 2.1 Habitat and major abiotic factors

- **Habitat** – the place where an organism lives; key physico-chemical (abiotic) components are **temperature, water, light and soil**.
- The **biotic components** (pathogens, parasites, predators, competitors) also form part of the habitat.
- **Niche** – the range of conditions an organism can tolerate, the resources it uses and its **distinct functional role** in the ecological system.

| Factor | Key points |
|---|---|
| **Temperature** | Most **ecologically relevant** factor. Affects **kinetics of enzymes**, basal metabolism and physiological functions. **Eurythermal** organisms tolerate a **wide range** of temperatures; **stenothermal** organisms are restricted to a **narrow range** (most organisms) |
| **Water** | Life on earth originated in water; productivity and distribution of plants depend on water. For aquatic organisms, **quality (salinity)** matters: **euryhaline** (wide salinity range) vs **stenohaline** (narrow range). Salinity (parts per thousand): **<5 inland waters, 30–35 sea, >100 hypersaline lagoons** |
| **Light** | Needed for photosynthesis; many plants flower only at certain **photoperiods**. Small herbs and shrubs of forests are adapted to **dim light** (shade plants). Many deep-ocean animals (>500 m) live in **perpetual darkness** |
| **Soil** | Nature and properties depend on climate, weathering, sedimentation. **Soil composition, grain size and aggregation** determine **percolation and water-holding capacity** → together with pH, mineral composition and topography, determine the **vegetation** |

### 2.2 Responses to abiotic factors

- **Homeostasis** – maintaining a constant internal environment despite varying external conditions.

| Response | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Regulate** | Maintain homeostasis by **physiological (and sometimes behavioural) means** | **All birds and mammals**, very few lower vertebrates and invertebrates (thermoregulation, osmoregulation). Humans: **sweating** in summer, **shivering** in winter |
| **Conform** | Body temperature/osmotic concentration **changes with the ambient** conditions | **99% of animals and nearly all plants**. Small animals (e.g., shrews, hummingbirds) are rare in polar regions – **large surface area relative to volume** → lose heat quickly |
| **Migrate** | Move **temporarily** from a stressful habitat to a more hospitable one | Birds migrating from **Siberia** to **Keoladeo National Park (Bharatpur)** in winter |
| **Suspend** | Avoid stress by suspending activity in time | **Bacteria, fungi, lower plants** – thick-walled spores; higher plants – **seed dormancy**; bears – **hibernation** in winter; snails and fish – **aestivation** in summer; zooplankton – **diapause** |

### 2.3 Adaptations

- **Adaptation** – any attribute (morphological, physiological, behavioural) that enables an organism to survive and reproduce in its habitat.

| Organism / group | Adaptation |
|---|---|
| **Kangaroo rat** (North American desert) | Meets water needs through **internal fat oxidation** (water is a by-product); concentrates urine → minimal water excretion |
| **Desert plants** | **Thick cuticle**, **sunken stomata**; **CAM pathway** (stomata open at night); leaves reduced to **spines**, photosynthesis by **flattened stems** (e.g., *Opuntia*) |
| **Mammals of colder climates** | **Shorter ears and limbs** to minimise heat loss (**Allen's rule**) |
| **Polar seals** | Thick layer of fat (**blubber**) below the skin – insulator |
| **Humans at high altitude** | **Altitude sickness** (nausea, fatigue, heart palpitations) due to low atmospheric pressure → less O<sub>2</sub>. Body compensates by **increasing RBC production**, decreasing binding affinity of haemoglobin, and increasing breathing rate |
| **Archaebacteria** | Flourish in **hot springs and deep-sea hydrothermal vents** (>100°C) |
| **Desert lizards** | **Behavioural** – bask in the sun when body temperature is low; move to shade when it rises; burrow in soil |

## 3. Population

- A **population** is a group of individuals of a species living in a **well-defined geographical area**, sharing or competing for **similar resources**, and **potentially interbreeding**.
- A group resulting from **asexual reproduction** is also generally considered a population for ecological studies.
- Examples: cormorants in a wetland, rats in an abandoned dwelling, teakwood trees in a forest tract, bacteria in a culture plate, lotus plants in a pond.
- Although an individual copes with a changed environment, **natural selection operates at the population level** → **population ecology links ecology to population genetics and evolution**.

### 3.1 Population attributes (attributes that individuals do NOT have)

1. **Birth rate** – **per capita births**
2. **Death rate** – **per capita deaths**
3. **Sex ratio** – e.g., 60% females, 40% males
4. **Age distribution** – shown as **age pyramids**
5. **Population density (N)**

> **Key Point: Worked examples from NCERT (frequently used in numericals)**
> - **Birth rate:** A pond had **20 lotus plants** last year; **8 new plants** added → birth rate = **8/20 = 0.4 offspring per lotus per year**.
> - **Death rate:** **4 of 40 fruitflies** died in a week → death rate = **4/40 = 0.1 individuals per fruitfly per week**.
> - Rates are always expressed **per capita** (per member of the population), not as total numbers.

#### Age pyramids

- Age distribution (per cent individuals of a given age or age group) plotted for the population → **age pyramid**.
- For humans, age pyramids show the age distribution of **males and females**.
- Three age groups: **pre-reproductive, reproductive and post-reproductive**.

| Shape | Status | Feature |
|---|---|---|
| **Triangular / broad base** | **Expanding (growing)** | Pre-reproductive individuals very large |
| **Bell-shaped** | **Stable** | Pre-reproductive and reproductive roughly equal |
| **Urn-shaped (narrow base)** | **Declining** | Pre-reproductive individuals fewer than reproductive |

#### Population density and its measurement

- **Population size = population density (N)**. Can be as low as **<10 (Siberian cranes at Bharatpur wetlands)** or millions (***Chlamydomonas*** in a pond).
- Need not be measured only in numbers:

| Situation | Best measure |
|---|---|
| Normal counting possible | **Total number** (most appropriate generally) |
| 200 *Parthenium* (carrot grass) plants vs a single huge banyan tree | **Per cent cover or biomass** (numbers would underestimate the banyan) |
| Huge populations (e.g., dense bacterial culture in a petri dish) | **Biomass / per cent cover** (counting impossible) |
| No need for absolute density | **Relative density** – e.g., **number of fish caught per trap** |
| Indirect estimation | **Tiger census** based on **pug marks and fecal pellets** |

### 3.2 Population growth

- Density fluctuates due to **four basic processes**:

| Process | Effect | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| **Natality (B)** | Increase | Number of **births** in a given period added to the initial density |
| **Immigration (I)** | Increase | Number of individuals **of the same species** that **come into the habitat** from elsewhere |
| **Mortality (D)** | Decrease | Number of **deaths** in the given period |
| **Emigration (E)** | Decrease | Number of individuals **who left the habitat** and went elsewhere |

> **Remember: Population growth equation**
> **N<sub>t+1</sub> = N<sub>t</sub> + [(B + I) – (D + E)]**
> Density increases if (B + I) > (D + E). Under **normal conditions, births and deaths** are the most important factors; immigration becomes important only under special conditions – e.g., when a **new habitat is just being colonised**, immigration may contribute more than birth rates.

### 3.3 Growth models

#### (i) Exponential (geometric) growth

- Occurs when **resources (food and space) are unlimited** – each species realises fully its **innate potential** to grow (as Darwin observed).
- If **b** = per capita birth rate and **d** = per capita death rate:
  - **dN/dt = (b – d) × N**
  - Let (b – d) = **r** → **dN/dt = rN**
- **r = intrinsic rate of natural increase** – very important parameter for assessing impacts of any biotic or abiotic factor on population growth.

| Organism | r value |
|---|---|
| **Norway rat** | **0.015** |
| **Flour beetle** | **0.12** |
| **Human population in India (1981)** | **0.0205** |

- Plotting N against time gives a **J-shaped curve**.
- **Integral form:** **N<sub>t</sub> = N<sub>0</sub> e<sup>rt</sup>**
  - N<sub>t</sub> = population density after time t; N<sub>0</sub> = population density at time zero; r = intrinsic rate of natural increase; e = base of natural logarithms (**2.71828**).
- Any species growing exponentially under unlimited resources can reach **enormous densities in a short time** – Darwin showed even the slow-growing **elephant** could reach enormous numbers. (The chessboard–wheat grain story; *Paramecium* doubling every day for 64 days.)

> **Exam Tip: Calculating r (NCERT exercise)**
> If a population doubles in 3 years: N<sub>t</sub> = 2N<sub>0</sub> → 2 = e<sup>3r</sup> → 3r = ln 2 = 0.693 → **r ≈ 0.231 per year**.

#### (ii) Logistic growth (Verhulst–Pearl)

- **No population in nature has unlimited resources** → competition among individuals → the "fittest" survive and reproduce.
- A given habitat has enough resources to support a **maximum possible number** – **carrying capacity (K)**.
- Growth phases: **lag phase → acceleration → deceleration → asymptote** (when N reaches K).
- Plotting N against time gives a **sigmoid (S-shaped) curve** – **Verhulst–Pearl logistic growth**.

> **Remember: Logistic equation**
> **dN/dt = rN [(K – N)/K]**
> N = population density at time t; r = intrinsic rate of natural increase; K = carrying capacity.
> When N is very small, (K – N)/K ≈ 1 → growth ≈ exponential. When N = K, growth = 0.

- Since resources for most animal populations are **finite and become limiting sooner or later**, the **logistic model is more realistic**.

| Feature | Exponential | Logistic |
|---|---|---|
| Resources | Unlimited | Limited |
| Curve | **J-shaped** | **S-shaped (sigmoid)** |
| Equation | dN/dt = rN | dN/dt = rN[(K – N)/K] |
| Carrying capacity | Not considered | **K** – asymptote |
| Realistic? | Less | **More** |

### 3.4 Life history variation

- Populations evolve to **maximise their reproductive fitness** – also called **Darwinian fitness (high r value)** – in their habitat.

| Strategy | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Breed only once** in lifetime | **Pacific salmon fish, bamboo** |
| **Breed many times** | **Most birds and mammals** |
| **Large number of small-sized offspring** | **Oysters, pelagic fishes** |
| **Small number of large-sized offspring** | **Birds, mammals** |

- Life history traits evolve in relation to **constraints imposed by abiotic and biotic components** of the habitat.

## 4. Population Interactions

- **No natural habitat** is inhabited by a **single species**. Minimum requirement – one more species on which it feeds; even a plant needs **soil microbes** (for nutrients) and **animal agents** (for pollination).
- **Interspecific interactions** – between populations of **two different species**; can be beneficial (+), detrimental (–) or neutral (0).

| Species A | Species B | Interaction |
|---|---|---|
| + | + | **Mutualism** |
| – | – | **Competition** |
| + | – | **Predation** |
| + | – | **Parasitism** |
| + | 0 | **Commensalism** |
| – | 0 | **Amensalism** |

- **Predation, parasitism and commensalism** share a common feature – **the interacting species live closely together**.

> **PYQ Trend**
> The +/–/0 table is asked almost every year, often as a "match the column" or "which interaction has + and 0". Remember: **amensalism = – / 0**, **commensalism = + / 0**.

### 4.1 Predation (+/–)

- **Nature's way of transferring energy** fixed by plants to **higher trophic levels**.
- A **sparrow eating seeds** is as much a predator as a tiger eating deer; **herbivores** are, in a broad ecological sense, not very different from predators.
- **Roles of predators:**
  1. Act as **"conduits" for energy transfer** across trophic levels.
  2. **Keep prey populations under control** – otherwise prey could reach very high densities → ecosystem instability.
     - **Prickly pear cactus** introduced into **Australia** in the **early 1920s** spread over millions of hectares of rangeland; controlled only after a **cactus-feeding predator (a moth)** from its natural habitat was introduced.
     - **Biological control** methods in agricultural pest control are based on the ability of the **predator to regulate prey population**.
  3. **Maintain species diversity** by **reducing the intensity of competition** among competing prey species.
     - **Starfish *Pisaster*** – important predator in **rocky intertidal communities of the American Pacific Coast**. When all starfish were removed from an enclosed area, **more than 10 species of invertebrates became extinct within a year** due to **interspecific competition**.
- **Prudent predators** – if a predator over-exploits its prey, the prey may become extinct and then the predator too; so predators in nature are "prudent".
- **Invasive species:** exotic species introduced into a new area spread fast because the invaded land **does not have their natural predators**.

#### Prey defences

| Defence | Example |
|---|---|
| **Camouflage (cryptic colouration)** | Some **insects and frogs** |
| **Poisonous** | Avoided by predators |
| **Distasteful chemicals** | **Monarch butterfly** – highly distasteful to its predator (**bird**) due to a special chemical acquired during its **caterpillar stage by feeding on a poisonous weed** |

#### Plant defences against herbivores

- **Nearly 25% of all insects are phytophagous** (feed on plant sap and other parts). Plants **cannot run away** from predators.
- **Morphological:** **thorns** (***Acacia*, cactus**) – most common.
- **Chemical:** chemicals that make the herbivore sick, inhibit feeding/digestion, disrupt reproduction or kill it.
  - ***Calotropis*** – produces highly poisonous **cardiac glycosides** → cattle and goats never browse on it.
  - Commercially extracted chemicals – **nicotine, caffeine, quinine, strychnine, opium** – are actually produced by plants **as defences against grazers and browsers**.

### 4.2 Competition (–/–)

- Darwin believed **interspecific competition is a potent force in organic evolution**.
- Common belief: competition occurs when **closely related species** compete for **limiting resources** – but this is **not entirely true**:
  1. **Totally unrelated species** can compete – e.g., in some shallow **South American lakes**, **visiting flamingoes and resident fishes** compete for common food, **zooplankton**.
  2. **Resources need not be limiting** – in **interference competition**, the feeding efficiency of one species is reduced by the **interfering and inhibitory presence** of another, even if resources are abundant.
- **Best definition:** competition is a process in which the **fitness of one species (measured by its 'r')** is **significantly lower in the presence of another species**.

#### Evidence of competition in nature

- **Laboratory:** **Gause** and others showed that when resources are limited, the **competitively superior species eventually eliminates the other**.
- **Abingdon tortoise** in the **Galapagos Islands** became **extinct within a decade** after **goats** were introduced – due to the goats' **greater browsing efficiency**.
- **Competitive release:** a species restricted to a small area by a superior competitor **expands its range dramatically** when the competitor is removed.
  - **Connell's field experiments** – on the **rocky sea coasts of Scotland**, the larger, competitively superior barnacle ***Balanus*** dominates the intertidal area and **excludes the smaller barnacle *Chthamalus*** from that zone.
- In general, **herbivores and plants** are more adversely affected by competition than **carnivores**.

> **Key Point: Gause's Competitive Exclusion Principle**
> Two **closely related species competing for the same resources cannot co-exist indefinitely**; the **competitively inferior one will be eliminated** eventually. True if resources are limiting, but not otherwise.

- **Resource partitioning** – mechanism promoting co-existence: species avoid competition by choosing **different times for feeding or different foraging patterns**.
  - **MacArthur** showed **five closely related species of warblers** living on the **same tree** co-existed due to **behavioural differences in foraging**.

### 4.3 Parasitism (+/–)

- Parasitic life ensures **free lodging and meals** → evolved in many groups from plants to higher vertebrates.
- Many parasites are **host-specific** → **host and parasite co-evolve**.
- **Adaptations of parasites:**
  - **Loss of unnecessary sense organs**
  - **Adhesive organs or suckers** to cling to the host
  - **Loss of digestive system**
  - **High reproductive capacity**
- Life cycles often complex, with **intermediate hosts or vectors**:
  - **Human liver fluke** (trematode) – **two intermediate hosts: a snail and a fish**.
  - **Malarial parasite** – needs a **vector (mosquito)**.
- Most parasites **harm the host** – reduce survival, growth, reproduction, population density; make the host **more vulnerable to predation**.

| Type | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Ectoparasites** | Feed on the **external surface** of the host | **Lice on humans, ticks on dogs**, **ectoparasitic copepods** on marine fish; ***Cuscuta*** – parasitic plant on hedge plants; has **lost chlorophyll and leaves** |
| **Endoparasites** | Live **inside** the host body (liver, kidney, lungs, RBCs) | More complex life cycles due to extreme specialisation; **simplified morphology and anatomy**, **high reproductive potential** |
| **Brood parasitism** | Parasitic bird **lays eggs in the host's nest** and lets the host incubate them | **Cuckoo (koel) and crow**; eggs of parasite **resemble the host's eggs in size and colour** |

> **Common Mistake**
> The **female mosquito is NOT considered a parasite**, though it needs our blood for reproduction – it does not live on/in the host and only visits it briefly for a blood meal.

### 4.4 Commensalism (+/0)

| Example | Who benefits | How |
|---|---|---|
| **Orchid** as an epiphyte on a **mango branch** | Orchid | Support; mango unaffected |
| **Barnacles** on the back of a **whale** | Barnacles | Whale unaffected |
| **Cattle egret** and **grazing cattle** | Egret | Cattle **stir up and flush out insects** from vegetation |
| **Clown fish** and **sea anemone** (with stinging tentacles) | Clown fish | **Protection from predators**; anemone derives no apparent benefit |

### 4.5 Mutualism (+/+)

- **Lichens** – intimate mutualism between a **fungus** and **photosynthesising algae or cyanobacteria**.
- **Mycorrhizae** – associations between **fungi and roots of higher plants**; fungi help in **absorption of essential nutrients**; plant gives fungi **energy-yielding carbohydrates**.
- **Plant–animal relationships** – most spectacular: plants need animals for **pollination and seed dispersal**; they pay "fees" – **pollen and nectar** for pollinators, **juicy and nutritious fruits** for seed dispersers. Also need safeguards against **"cheaters"** (nectar thieves) → **co-evolution** of mutualists.

#### Fig and wasp

- Many fig species have a **tight one-to-one relationship** with a pollinator **wasp species**.
- The **female wasp** uses the fruit as an **oviposition (egg-laying) site** and the **developing seeds** to nourish its larvae.
- The wasp **pollinates the fig inflorescence** while searching for egg-laying sites; the fig offers some **developing seeds** as food for wasp larvae.

#### Orchids and *Ophrys*

- Orchids show diverse floral patterns evolved to attract the right pollinator (**bees and bumblebees**).
- **Mediterranean orchid *Ophrys*** employs **"sexual deceit"**:
  - One **petal resembles the female bee** in size, colour and markings.
  - The **male bee "pseudocopulates"** with the flower → gets dusted with pollen → transfers it to another flower.
  - If the female bee's colour pattern changes during evolution, the orchid must **co-evolve** to maintain the resemblance.

### 4.6 Amensalism (–/0)

- One species is **harmed** while the other is **unaffected** (e.g., *Penicillium* secreting penicillin that kills bacteria – a standard example beyond NCERT text).
