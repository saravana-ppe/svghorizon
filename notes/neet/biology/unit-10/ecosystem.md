## 1. Ecosystem – Structure and Function

- **Ecosystem** – a **functional unit of nature** where living organisms interact among themselves and with the surrounding physical environment.
- Size varies from a **small pond** to a **large forest or sea**. The entire **biosphere** is regarded as a **global ecosystem**.

| Category | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Terrestrial** | Forest, grassland, desert |
| **Aquatic** | Pond, lake, wetland, river, estuary |
| **Man-made** | Crop fields, aquarium |

### 1.1 Structure

- Interaction of biotic and abiotic components results in a **physical structure** characteristic of each ecosystem.
- **Species composition** – identification and enumeration of plant and animal species.
- **Stratification** – **vertical distribution** of species at different levels: **trees – top strata**, **shrubs – second**, **herbs and grasses – bottom layers** of a forest.

### 1.2 Functional aspects (four)

1. **Productivity**
2. **Decomposition**
3. **Energy flow**
4. **Nutrient cycling**

### 1.3 Pond as an ecosystem (model example)

| Component | Constituents |
|---|---|
| **Abiotic** | **Water** with dissolved inorganic and organic substances; **rich soil deposit** at the bottom. Solar input, cycle of temperature, day-length and climate **regulate the rate of function** |
| **Autotrophs (producers)** | **Phytoplankton**, some algae, and floating, submerged and marginal plants |
| **Consumers** | **Zooplankton**, free-swimming and bottom-dwelling forms |
| **Decomposers** | **Fungi, bacteria and flagellates** – especially abundant **at the bottom** |

- Functions performed: conversion of inorganic → organic material by autotrophs using sunlight; consumption of autotrophs by heterotrophs; decomposition and **mineralisation** of dead matter for reuse.
- **Unidirectional movement of energy** towards higher trophic levels and its **dissipation as heat**.

## 2. Productivity

- **Primary production** – the **amount of biomass or organic matter produced per unit area over a time period by plants during photosynthesis**. Expressed as weight (**g m<sup>–2</sup>**) or energy (**kcal m<sup>–2</sup>**).
- **Productivity** – the **rate of biomass production**. Expressed as **g m<sup>–2</sup> yr<sup>–1</sup>** or **(kcal m<sup>–2</sup>) yr<sup>–1</sup>** – used to compare different ecosystems.

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Gross primary productivity (GPP)** | **Rate of production of organic matter during photosynthesis** |
| **Net primary productivity (NPP)** | **GPP minus respiration losses (R)**: **GPP – R = NPP**. NPP is the **biomass available for consumption by heterotrophs** (herbivores and decomposers) |
| **Secondary productivity** | **Rate of formation of new organic matter by consumers** |

- Primary productivity depends on **plant species**, **environmental factors**, **availability of nutrients** and **photosynthetic capacity** – so it varies among ecosystems.

> **Remember: Global productivity figures**
> - Annual NPP of the **whole biosphere ≈ 170 billion tons (dry weight)** of organic matter.
> - **Oceans** – occupy about **70%** of the surface but produce only **55 billion tons**; the rest (about 115 billion tons) is on **land**.
> - Main reason for low ocean productivity: **light** decreases with depth and **nutrients (especially nitrogen)** are limiting.

## 3. Decomposition

- **Earthworm** – the "**farmer's friend**" – breaks down complex organic matter and **loosens the soil**.
- **Decomposition** – breakdown of complex organic matter into **inorganic substances like CO<sub>2</sub>, water and nutrients** by **decomposers**.
- **Detritus** – dead plant remains (**leaves, bark, flowers**) and dead remains of animals, including **fecal matter** – the **raw material** for decomposition.

### 3.1 Steps in decomposition

| Step | Process | Agent |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Fragmentation** | Breakdown of detritus into **smaller particles** | **Detritivores** (e.g., **earthworm**) |
| **2. Leaching** | **Water-soluble inorganic nutrients** go down into the soil horizon and get **precipitated as unavailable salts** | Water (physical process) |
| **3. Catabolism** | **Bacterial and fungal enzymes** degrade detritus into **simpler inorganic substances** | Bacteria, fungi |
| **4. Humification** | Accumulation of a **dark-coloured amorphous substance – humus** – **highly resistant to microbial action**, decomposes extremely slowly; **colloidal** → **reservoir of nutrients** | In soil |
| **5. Mineralisation** | Humus further degraded by some microbes → **release of inorganic nutrients** | Microbes |

- **All the steps operate simultaneously** on the detritus.

### 3.2 Factors affecting the rate of decomposition

- Decomposition is largely an **oxygen-requiring** process.
- Controlled by **chemical composition of detritus** and **climatic factors**:

| Faster decomposition | Slower decomposition |
|---|---|
| Detritus rich in **nitrogen** and **water-soluble substances like sugars** | Detritus rich in **lignin and chitin** |
| **Warm and moist** environment | **Low temperature** and **anaerobiosis** (→ build-up of organic material) |

- **Temperature and soil moisture** are the **most important climatic factors**, acting through their effects on **soil microbes**.

> **Common Mistake**
> Leaching makes nutrients **unavailable** (precipitated salts) – it does not make them more available. Also, **humus decomposes very slowly**, not quickly.

## 4. Energy Flow

- **Sun is the only source of energy** for all ecosystems on Earth, **except the deep-sea hydrothermal ecosystem**.
- Of the incident solar radiation, **less than 50% is photosynthetically active radiation (PAR)**.
- Plants capture only **2–10% of the PAR** – this small amount sustains the entire living world.
- **Unidirectional flow** of energy: **sun → producers → consumers**.
- Ecosystems obey the **Second Law of Thermodynamics** – need a constant supply of energy to synthesise molecules and counteract the tendency toward **increasing disorderliness**.

### 4.1 Producers and consumers

- **Producers** – green plants. Terrestrial: **herbaceous and woody plants**; aquatic: **phytoplankton, algae and higher plants**.
- **Consumers (heterotrophs)** – depend on plants directly or indirectly.

| Consumer | Feeds on | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary consumers (herbivores)** | Producers | **Insects, birds, mammals** (terrestrial); **molluscs** (aquatic) |
| **Secondary consumers (primary carnivores)** | Herbivores | – |
| **Tertiary consumers (secondary carnivores)** | Primary carnivores | – |

### 4.2 Food chains

| Feature | Grazing food chain (GFC) | Detritus food chain (DFC) |
|---|---|---|
| Starts with | **Living producers (plants)** | **Dead organic matter (detritus)** |
| Example | **Grass → Goat → Man** | Detritus → decomposers |
| Main organisms | Herbivores, carnivores | **Decomposers – mainly fungi and bacteria (saprotrophs)** |
| Importance | **Major conduit for energy flow in aquatic ecosystems** | **In terrestrial ecosystems, a much larger fraction of energy flows through DFC** |

- **Saprotrophs** (*sapro* = to decompose) secrete **digestive enzymes** that break down dead and waste materials into simple inorganic materials, which they **absorb**.
- DFC may be connected with GFC at some levels; **omnivores** like **cockroaches and crows** → natural interconnection of food chains forms a **food web**.
- **Death of an organism** is the beginning of the **detritus food chain/web**.

### 4.3 Trophic levels

- **Trophic level** – the specific place an organism occupies in a food chain based on its **source of nutrition**.
  - **1st** – producers; **2nd** – herbivores (primary consumers); **3rd** – carnivores (secondary consumers); **4th** – top carnivores.
- The **amount of energy decreases at successive trophic levels**.
- **Standing crop** – the **mass of living material** at each trophic level at a particular time; measured as **biomass** or **number** per unit area.
  - Biomass is expressed as fresh or **dry weight** – **dry weight is more accurate** (water content varies).
- **10 per cent law** (Lindeman) – **only 10% of the energy** is transferred to each trophic level from the lower trophic level → **restricts the number of trophic levels** in the grazing food chain.

> **Exam Tip: 10% law calculation**
> If producers have **10,000 J**, then herbivores get **1,000 J**, primary carnivores **100 J**, secondary carnivores **10 J**. Questions often give the amount at one level and ask for another.

## 5. Ecological Pyramids

- The relationship between organisms at different trophic levels expressed in terms of **number, biomass or energy**.
- **Base** – producers (first trophic level); **apex** – tertiary or top-level consumer.
- Any calculation must include **all organisms at that trophic level**.
- A **trophic level represents a functional level, not a species** – the same species may occupy **more than one trophic level** at the same time (e.g., **sparrow** – primary consumer when it eats seeds, fruits, peas; secondary consumer when it eats insects and worms).

| Pyramid | Usually | Exceptions (inverted) |
|---|---|---|
| **Number** | Upright – e.g., **grassland**: about **6 million plants** support only **3 top carnivores** | **Inverted in a tree ecosystem** – one big tree supports many insects, which support small birds, then fewer large birds (also **spindle-shaped / partly inverted**) |
| **Biomass** | Upright – sharp decrease in biomass at higher trophic levels | **Inverted in the sea** – **small standing crop of phytoplankton supports a large standing crop of zooplankton/fishes** (biomass of fishes far exceeds that of phytoplankton) |
| **Energy** | **Always upright – can never be inverted** | None – energy is always lost as **heat** at each step |

- In the ideal energy pyramid, **primary producers convert only 1% of the energy in sunlight** available to them into **NPP**.
- Each bar in an energy pyramid = energy at each trophic level **in a given time or annually per unit area**.

> **Remember: Limitations of ecological pyramids**
> 1. **Do not take into account the same species belonging to two or more trophic levels.**
> 2. **Assume a simple food chain** – which almost never exists in nature; **do not accommodate a food web**.
> 3. **Saprophytes are not given any place** in ecological pyramids, even though they play a vital role.

## 6. Nutrient Cycling and Ecosystem Services (brief – from NCERT summary)

- **Nutrient cycling** – storage and movement of nutrient elements through the various components of the ecosystem; nutrients are **used repeatedly**.

| Type | Reservoir | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Gaseous cycle** | **Atmosphere or hydrosphere** | **Carbon** (and nitrogen) |
| **Sedimentary cycle** | **Earth's crust** | **Phosphorus** (and sulphur) |

- **Ecosystem services** – products of ecosystem processes, e.g., **purification of air and water by forests**, pollination, pest control, flood and soil-erosion control, climate moderation.
