- Main areas: **therapeutics, diagnostics, genetically modified crops for agriculture, processed food, bioremediation, waste treatment and energy production**.
- Three **critical research areas** of biotechnology:
  1. Providing the **best catalyst** in the form of improved organism (usually a microbe or pure enzyme)
  2. Creating **optimal conditions** through engineering for a catalyst to act
  3. **Downstream processing** technologies to purify the protein/organic compound

## 1. Biotechnological Applications in Agriculture

Three options for increasing food production:

1. **Agro-chemical based agriculture**
2. **Organic agriculture**
3. **Genetically engineered crop-based agriculture**

- The **Green Revolution** tripled food supply, but was not enough; it depended on **improved crop varieties, agrochemicals (fertilisers and pesticides)**, which are expensive and harmful to the environment.
- Solution: **genetically modified crops**.

### 1.1 Tissue culture and allied techniques

- **Explant** – any part of a plant taken out and grown in a test tube under sterile conditions in special nutrient media.
- **Totipotency** – capacity to generate a whole plant from any cell/explant.
- **Micropropagation** – producing **thousands of plants in a very short time** through tissue culture.
- **Somaclones** – plants **genetically identical** to the original plant (e.g., **tomato, banana, apple** produced on a commercial scale).
- **Virus-free plants** – although the plant is infected, the **meristem (apical and axillary)** is free of virus → meristem culture gives virus-free plants (**banana, sugarcane, potato**).
- **Somatic hybridisation** – single cells isolated from plants, **cell walls digested** to get naked **protoplasts**; protoplasts of two varieties fused → **somatic hybrids**. Example: **pomato** (tomato + potato protoplast fusion) – but it does not have all desired characteristics.

### 1.2 Genetically Modified Organisms (GMOs)

- Plants, bacteria, fungi and animals whose **genes have been altered by manipulation** are **GMOs**.
- Advantages of GM plants:
  1. **Tolerance against abiotic stresses** (cold, drought, salt, heat)
  2. **Reduced reliance on chemical pesticides** (pest-resistant crops)
  3. **Reduced post-harvest losses**
  4. **Increased efficiency of mineral usage** by plants (prevents early exhaustion of soil fertility)
  5. **Enhanced nutritional value** of food – e.g., **golden rice**, i.e., **Vitamin 'A' enriched rice**
- GM plants also used to create **tailor-made plants** to supply alternative resources to industries: **starches, fuels and pharmaceuticals**.

### 1.3 Pest-resistant plants – Bt crops

- **Bt toxin** is produced by the bacterium ***Bacillus thuringiensis*** (Bt for short).
- The Bt toxin gene has been cloned from the bacteria and **expressed in plants** to provide resistance to insects **without the need for insecticides** – creating a **bio-pesticide**.
- Examples: **Bt cotton, Bt corn, rice, tomato, potato and soyabean**.

#### Mechanism of Bt toxin action

- Some strains of *B. thuringiensis* produce proteins that kill certain insects:

| Insect group | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Lepidopterans** | **Tobacco budworm, armyworm** |
| **Coleopterans** | **Beetles** |
| **Dipterans** | **Flies, mosquitoes** |

- *B. thuringiensis* forms **protein crystals** during a particular phase of growth – containing a **toxic insecticidal protein**.
- **Why doesn't the toxin kill the bacterium?** The Bt toxin protein exists as **inactive protoxins**.
- Once an insect ingests the inactive toxin, it is **converted into an active form** of toxin due to the **alkaline pH of the gut**, which **solubilises the crystals**.
- The activated toxin **binds to the surface of midgut epithelial cells** and **creates pores** → **cell swelling and lysis** → **death of the insect**.

#### Specific Bt toxin genes

- Bt toxin is coded by a gene **cry**; there are a number of them.
- Specificity: the proteins encoded by different *cry* genes are toxic to different insects.

| Gene | Controls |
|---|---|
| ***cryIAc*** and ***cryIIAb*** | **Cotton bollworms** |
| ***cryIAb*** | **Corn borer** |

> **Exam Tip**
> "cry**IAc** and cry**IIAb** → **c**otton bollworms"; "cry**IAb** → corn **b**orer". The name of the gene is written in italics and lowercase (*cry*), while the protein is written with a capital C (Cry).

### 1.4 Pest-resistant plants – RNA interference (RNAi)

- A nematode, ***Meloidogyne incognita***, infects the **roots of tobacco plants** and causes a great **reduction in yield**.
- Strategy based on **RNA interference (RNAi)**:
  - RNAi takes place in **all eukaryotic organisms** as a method of **cellular defence**.
  - Involves **silencing of a specific mRNA** due to a **complementary double-stranded RNA (dsRNA)** molecule that binds to and **prevents translation** of the mRNA.
  - Source of complementary RNA – infection by **viruses having RNA genomes** or **mobile genetic elements (transposons)** that replicate via an RNA intermediate.
- Using ***Agrobacterium* vectors**, **nematode-specific genes** were introduced into the host plant.
- The introduced DNA produced **both sense and anti-sense RNA** in the host cells.
- These two RNAs, being complementary, formed a **dsRNA** → **initiated RNAi** → **silenced the specific mRNA of the nematode**.
- Result: the parasite **could not survive** in a transgenic host expressing specific interfering RNA → the transgenic plant got **protected from the parasite**.

> **PYQ Trend**
> Frequently asked: name of the nematode (*Meloidogyne incognita*), the host (tobacco), the vector (*Agrobacterium*), and the fact that **dsRNA silences mRNA** of the parasite (not of the plant).

## 2. Biotechnological Applications in Medicine

- rDNA technology enables **mass production of safe and more effective therapeutic drugs**.
- Recombinant therapeutics **do not induce unwanted immunological responses** (as seen with similar products isolated from non-human sources).
- At present, about **30 recombinant therapeutics** have been **approved for human use** the world over; **in India, 12** of these are presently being marketed.

### 2.1 Genetically engineered insulin

- **Management of adult-onset diabetes** is possible by taking insulin at regular intervals.
- Earlier, insulin was extracted from the **pancreas of slaughtered cattle and pigs** → caused **allergy or other types of reactions** to the foreign protein in some patients.
- **Structure of insulin:**
  - Consists of **two short polypeptide chains – chain A and chain B** – **linked together by disulphide bridges**.
  - In mammals, insulin is synthesised as a **pro-hormone** (like a pro-enzyme) which contains an extra stretch called the **C peptide**.
  - The **C peptide is not present in mature insulin** and is **removed during maturation** into insulin.
- **Main challenge:** getting insulin assembled into a mature form.
- In **1983**, **Eli Lilly**, an American company, prepared **two DNA sequences corresponding to A and B chains** of human insulin and **introduced them in plasmids of *E. coli*** to produce insulin chains.
- **Chains A and B were produced separately**, extracted and **combined by creating disulphide bonds** to form **human insulin**.

> **Common Mistake**
> Recombinant insulin (Eli Lilly) was made by producing **A and B chains separately** – NOT by producing pro-insulin and removing C-peptide. Also, insulin is *not* made in yeast in the NCERT description – the host is ***E. coli***.

### 2.2 Vaccine production (syllabus link)

- rDNA technology allows production of **antigenic polypeptides of pathogens in bacteria or yeast** – these are used as vaccines (e.g., **Hepatitis B vaccine produced from yeast**) – covered under immunology in Unit 8.
- Transgenic animals are used to **test the safety of vaccines** (e.g., **polio vaccine** tested on transgenic mice – see Transgenic Animals, below).

### 2.3 Gene therapy

- **Gene therapy** – collection of methods that allows **correction of a gene defect diagnosed in a child/embryo**.
- Genes are **inserted into a person's cells and tissues** to treat a disease.
- Correction involves **delivery of a normal gene** into the individual or embryo to **take over the function of** and **compensate for the non-functional gene**.
- **First clinical gene therapy** – in **1990** to a **4-year-old girl** with **adenosine deaminase (ADA) deficiency**.
  - The **ADA enzyme is crucial for the immune system** to function.
  - The disorder is caused by **deletion of the gene for adenosine deaminase**.

| Treatment approach | Details | Permanent? |
|---|---|---|
| **Bone marrow transplantation** | Some children cured | – |
| **Enzyme replacement therapy** | Functional ADA given by injection | **Not completely curative** |
| **Gene therapy (1990)** | **Lymphocytes** from patient's blood grown in culture outside the body → functional **ADA cDNA** introduced using a **retroviral vector** → lymphocytes returned to the patient | **No** – lymphocytes are **not immortal**, so patient needs **periodic infusion** of genetically engineered lymphocytes |
| **Permanent cure (possible)** | Gene from marrow cells producing ADA introduced into cells at **early embryonic stages** | **Yes** |

> **PYQ Trend**
> ADA deficiency, year 1990, 4-year-old girl, **lymphocytes**, **retroviral vector**, **cDNA**, and the reason for periodic infusion (lymphocytes are not immortal) – each of these has been asked.

### 2.4 Molecular diagnosis

- Conventional methods (**serum and urine analysis**) cannot detect a disease early.
- Early diagnosis is possible by:

| Technique | Principle / Use |
|---|---|
| **PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction)** | Very low concentration of a pathogen can be detected by **amplification of its nucleic acid**. Used to detect **HIV in suspected AIDS patients**, to detect **mutations in genes in suspected cancer patients**, and to detect many other **genetic disorders** |
| **Recombinant DNA technology / probes** | A **single-stranded DNA or RNA tagged with a radioactive molecule (probe)** is allowed to hybridise to its complementary DNA in a clone of cells, followed by **detection using autoradiography**. The clone with the **mutated gene will not appear** on the photographic film because the probe will not be complementary to the mutated gene |
| **ELISA (Enzyme-Linked Immuno-Sorbent Assay)** | Based on the principle of **antigen–antibody interaction**. Infection by a pathogen detected by the presence of **antigens (proteins, glycoproteins)** or by detecting **antibodies** synthesised against the pathogen |

## 3. Transgenic Animals

- Animals that have had their **DNA manipulated to possess and express an extra (foreign) gene** are **transgenic animals**.
- Transgenic **rats, rabbits, pigs, sheep, cows and fish** have been produced; however **over 95% of all existing transgenic animals are mice**.

### Why are transgenic animals produced?

1. **Normal physiology and development** – to study how genes are regulated and how they affect normal functions (e.g., study of complex factors involved in growth such as **insulin-like growth factor**).
2. **Study of disease** – designed to **increase our understanding of how genes contribute to development of disease**; transgenic models exist for **cancer, cystic fibrosis, rheumatoid arthritis and Alzheimer's**.
3. **Biological products** – medicines containing biological products are expensive; transgenic animals make useful biological products:
   - **Human protein (α-1-antitrypsin)** used to treat **emphysema**.
   - Attempts for treatment of **phenylketonuria (PKU)** and **cystic fibrosis**.
   - **Rosie** – first transgenic **cow**, **1997**, produced **human protein-enriched milk (2.4 grams per litre)**. The milk contained the **human alpha-lactalbumin** and was **nutritionally a more balanced product for human babies** than natural cow milk.
4. **Vaccine safety** – transgenic **mice** are being developed to test the safety of vaccines before use on humans (e.g., **polio vaccine**). Could replace the use of **monkeys** for testing.
5. **Chemical safety testing (toxicity/safety testing)** – transgenic animals carrying genes that make them **more sensitive to toxic substances** than non-transgenic animals; results obtained in **less time**.

> **Exam Tip: Rosie**
> Rosie – cow – **1997** – **human α-lactalbumin** – **2.4 g/L**. Don't confuse with Dolly (the sheep, a clone – not transgenic, not in current NCERT).

## 4. Ethical Issues, Biosafety, Biopiracy and Patents

- Manipulation of living organisms by humans cannot go on without **regulation**. Ethical standards are needed to evaluate morality of all human activities that might help or harm living organisms.
- Biological significance also matters: **GMOs may have unpredictable results** when introduced into the ecosystem.

### 4.1 GEAC

- The Indian Government set up the **Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee (GEAC)**, which:
  - Makes decisions regarding the **validity of GM research**
  - Assesses the **safety of introducing GM organisms for public services**

### 4.2 Patents and biopiracy

- **Patent** – right granted by a government to an inventor to **prevent others from commercial use** of the invention.
- Concern: patents granted for products and technologies that make use of **genetic materials, plants and other biological resources** that have long been identified, developed and used by farmers and indigenous people of a specific region/country.

> **Remember: Basmati case**
> - In **1997**, an **American company** got **patent rights on Basmati rice** through the **US Patent and Trademark Office**.
> - This allowed the company to sell a "new" variety of Basmati in the US and abroad.
> - The "new" variety had actually been derived from **Indian farmers' varieties** – **Indian Basmati was crossed with semi-dwarf varieties** and claimed as an invention or novelty.
> - India has an estimated **2,00,000 varieties of rice**, of which **27 are documented varieties of Basmati**.

- Other attempts to patent Indian traditional herbal medicines: **turmeric and neem**.
- **Biopiracy** – use of bio-resources by **multinational companies and other organisations** **without proper authorisation** from the countries and people concerned **without compensatory payment**.
- Most industrialised nations are **rich financially but poor in biodiversity and traditional knowledge**; developing and underdeveloped countries are **rich in biodiversity and traditional knowledge**.
- Need to develop laws to prevent unauthorised exploitation of bio-resources and traditional knowledge; there is growing realisation of **injustice, inadequate compensation and benefit sharing** between developed and developing nations.
- **Indian Parliament** cleared the **second amendment of the Indian Patents Bill**, which takes such issues into consideration, including **patent terms, emergency provisions and research and development initiatives**.
