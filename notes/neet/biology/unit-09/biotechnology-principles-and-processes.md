## 1. What is Biotechnology?

- **Biotechnology** deals with techniques of using **live organisms or enzymes from organisms** to produce products and processes useful to humans.
- Old forms: making curd, bread or wine (microbe-mediated processes) – also biotechnology in a broad sense.
- In a **restricted sense** today it refers to processes using **genetically modified organisms** on a large scale.
- Other areas under biotechnology: *in vitro* fertilisation leading to a "test-tube" baby, synthesising a gene and using it, developing a DNA vaccine, correcting a defective gene.

> **Remember: EFB definition**
> The **European Federation of Biotechnology (EFB)** defines biotechnology as "the **integration of natural science and organisms, cells, parts thereof, and molecular analogues** for products and services". It covers both traditional and modern molecular biotechnology.

## 2. Principles of Biotechnology

Modern biotechnology rests on **two core techniques**:

| Technique | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Genetic engineering** | Alters the **chemistry of genetic material (DNA and RNA)**, introduces it into host organisms and thus **changes the phenotype** of the host |
| **Bioprocess engineering** | Maintains a **sterile (microbial contamination-free) ambience** in chemical engineering processes so that only the desired microbe/eukaryotic cell grows in **large quantities** for manufacturing antibiotics, vaccines, enzymes, etc. |

### 2.1 Why genetic engineering is better than traditional hybridisation

- Traditional hybridisation (plant and animal breeding) often leads to **inclusion and multiplication of undesirable genes** along with the desired ones.
- Genetic engineering allows us to **isolate and introduce only one or a set of desirable genes** without introducing undesirable genes into the target organism.

### 2.2 Why a gene must be linked to an origin of replication

- A piece of DNA introduced into an alien organism is usually **not able to multiply itself** in the progeny cells.
- It can multiply only when it **integrates into the host genome** or is part of a chromosome that has an ***ori*** (origin of replication).
- The ***ori*** is a specific DNA sequence responsible for **initiating replication**. It also **controls the copy number** of the linked DNA.
- So the alien DNA is linked to the *ori* – it can then replicate and multiply in the host. This is also called **cloning** (making multiple identical copies of any template DNA).

### 2.3 The first recombinant DNA

- Made in **1972** by **Stanley Cohen and Herbert Boyer**.
- They isolated an **antibiotic resistance gene** by cutting out a piece of DNA from a **plasmid of *Salmonella typhimurium***.
- The gene was linked to a plasmid vector using **DNA ligase** ("molecular glue") after cutting with **restriction enzymes** ("molecular scissors").
- The recombinant plasmid was transferred into ***Escherichia coli*** → the gene got replicated with the host DNA (cloning of the antibiotic resistance gene in *E. coli*).

> **Key Point: Three basic steps in genetically modifying an organism**
> 1. **Identification of DNA with desirable genes**
> 2. **Introduction of the identified DNA into the host**
> 3. **Maintenance of introduced DNA in the host and transfer of the DNA to its progeny**

## 3. Tools of Recombinant DNA Technology

Key tools: **restriction enzymes, polymerase enzymes, ligases, vectors and the host organism**.

### 3.1 Restriction enzymes ("molecular scissors")

- In **1963**, two enzymes responsible for **restricting the growth of bacteriophage in *E. coli*** were isolated:
  - one **added methyl groups** to DNA (methylase);
  - the other **cut DNA** – called **restriction endonuclease**.
- The first restriction endonuclease – ***Hind* II** – always cut DNA at a particular point by recognising a specific sequence of **six base pairs**. This is its **recognition sequence**.
- Today **more than 900 restriction enzymes** have been isolated from **over 230 strains of bacteria**, each recognising a different recognition sequence.
- Restriction enzymes belong to a larger class called **nucleases**:

| Nuclease type | Action |
|---|---|
| **Exonuclease** | Removes nucleotides from the **ends** of DNA |
| **Endonuclease** | Makes cuts at **specific positions within** the DNA |

- Each restriction endonuclease **inspects the length of a DNA sequence**; on finding its specific recognition sequence it binds to the DNA and **cuts each of the two strands** at specific points in their **sugar–phosphate backbones**.

#### Naming of restriction enzymes (very frequently asked)

- **First letter** – from the **genus** (capital); **next two letters** – from the **species** of the prokaryotic cell from which it was isolated.
- Then the **strain** letter, and a **Roman numeral** indicating the **order in which the enzyme was isolated** from that strain.

> **Exam Tip: EcoRI**
> **E** = *Escherichia* (genus), **co** = *coli* (species), **R** = strain **RY 13**, **I** = first enzyme isolated from this strain. Remember: "R" is the strain, NOT "restriction".

#### Palindromic nucleotide sequences

- Restriction endonucleases recognise **palindromic sequences** – the sequence reads the **same on the two strands** when the **orientation of reading is kept the same** (both 5'→3' or both 3'→5').
- Example (recognition site of EcoRI):

| Strand | Sequence |
|---|---|
| Upper | 5' — **GAATTC** — 3' |
| Lower | 3' — **CTTAAG** — 5' |

- Reading both strands 5'→3' gives GAATTC in both – a palindrome.

#### Sticky ends

- Restriction enzymes cut the strand **a little away from the centre of the palindrome**, but **between the same two bases on opposite strands** (EcoRI cuts between **G and A**).
- This leaves **single-stranded overhanging stretches** at the ends – called **sticky ends**.
- Sticky ends form **hydrogen bonds** with complementary cut counterparts – this **stickiness facilitates the action of DNA ligase**.
- **Important:** when a **vector and source DNA are cut with the same restriction enzyme**, the resultant fragments have the **same kind of sticky ends**, which can be joined end-to-end using **DNA ligase**.

> **Common Mistake**
> Restriction enzymes cut the **phosphodiester bonds** (sugar–phosphate backbone), not hydrogen bonds. DNA ligase joins fragments by forming **phosphodiester bonds**.

### 3.2 Separation and isolation of DNA fragments – Gel electrophoresis

- DNA fragments produced by restriction digestion are separated by **gel electrophoresis**.
- DNA is **negatively charged** → fragments move **towards the anode (positive electrode)** under an electric field.
- Matrix: most commonly **agarose** – a natural polymer extracted from **sea weeds**.
- Fragments separate (resolve) **according to their size** by the **sieving effect** of the agarose gel – **smaller fragments move farther**.
- Visualisation: stained with **ethidium bromide** followed by exposure to **UV radiation** → **bright orange-coloured bands** (pure DNA cannot be seen in visible light without staining).
- Separated bands are **cut out from the agarose gel** and extracted from the gel piece – called **elution**.
- The purified DNA fragments are used to construct recombinant DNA by joining with cloning vectors.

> **PYQ Trend**
> Questions ask: direction of movement (anode), matrix source (sea weed), stain (ethidium bromide + UV → orange bands), and "smaller fragments move farther". Remember in a gel with lanes, the band **nearest the well** is the **largest** fragment.

### 3.3 Cloning vectors

- **Plasmids and bacteriophages** can replicate within bacterial cells **independent of the chromosomal DNA**.
  - Bacteriophages – very high number per cell → **high copy number** of their genome.
  - Some plasmids have only **1–2 copies** per cell; others **15–100 copies**.
- When a piece of alien DNA is linked to these vectors, it is **multiplied to the copy number of the vector**.

#### Features required to facilitate cloning into a vector

1. **Origin of replication (*ori*)**
   - Sequence from where **replication starts**; any DNA linked to it can replicate in the host.
   - Also responsible for **controlling the copy number** of the linked DNA. To recover many copies of target DNA, clone it in a vector whose *ori* supports **high copy number**.
2. **Selectable marker**
   - Helps in **identifying and eliminating non-transformants** and **selectively permitting growth of transformants**.
   - **Transformation** – procedure through which a piece of DNA is introduced into a host bacterium.
   - Normally genes encoding **resistance to antibiotics** such as **ampicillin, chloramphenicol, tetracycline or kanamycin** are used (normal *E. coli* cells do not carry resistance to these).
3. **Cloning sites**
   - To link alien DNA, the vector should have **very few, preferably single, recognition sites** for commonly used restriction enzymes.
   - **More than one recognition site** within the vector generates **several fragments** and complicates gene cloning.
   - Ligation of alien DNA is carried out at a restriction site present in **one of the two antibiotic resistance genes**.
4. **Vectors for cloning genes in plants and animals** (see below)

#### pBR322 – the most frequently asked vector

| Feature | Details |
|---|---|
| Nature | *E. coli* cloning vector (plasmid) |
| *ori* | Present |
| *rop* | Codes for proteins involved in **replication of the plasmid** |
| Antibiotic resistance genes | ***amp<sup>R</sup>*** (ampicillin resistance) and ***tet<sup>R</sup>*** (tetracycline resistance) |
| Restriction sites | ***Hind* III, *Eco*RI, *Bam*HI, *Sal*I, *Pvu*II, *Pst*I, *Cla*I** |
| Sites within *tet<sup>R</sup>* | ***Bam*HI** and ***Sal*I** |
| Sites within *amp<sup>R</sup>* | ***Pvu*I** and ***Pst*I** |

> **Exam Tip**
> "Which site is present in *tet<sup>R</sup>* of pBR322?" – **BamHI or SalI**. "Which in *amp<sup>R</sup>*?" – **PstI or PvuI**. **EcoRI, HindIII, ClaI, PvuII** lie outside both resistance genes. The *rop* gene codes for proteins for **plasmid replication** – not for antibiotic resistance.

#### Insertional inactivation – selecting recombinants

- **Example with pBR322:** Insert foreign DNA at the **BamHI site of the tetracycline resistance gene**.
  - Recombinant plasmid **loses tetracycline resistance** but still has ampicillin resistance.
  - All transformants grow on **ampicillin**-containing medium.
  - Then transfer them (replica plating) to **tetracycline**-containing medium:
    - **Recombinants grow on ampicillin but NOT on tetracycline.**
    - **Non-recombinants grow on both.**
  - Here one antibiotic resistance gene helps **select transformants**, and the other gene, **inactivated by insertion**, helps **select recombinants**.
- This **simultaneous plating on two plates** is **cumbersome** → alternative selectable markers were developed based on **production of colour in the presence of a chromogenic substrate**.

#### Blue–white selection (β-galactosidase)

- Recombinant DNA is inserted **within the coding sequence of the enzyme β-galactosidase**.
- This **inactivates the gene** – called **insertional inactivation**.
- On a medium with a **chromogenic substrate**:

| Type of colony | β-galactosidase | Colour |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-recombinant** (no insert) | Active | **Blue** colonies |
| **Recombinant** (insert present) | Inactivated | **Colourless / white** colonies |

> **Common Mistake**
> Students often reverse this: **blue = non-recombinant**, **white = recombinant**. The insert *inactivates* the enzyme, so no blue colour is produced.

#### Vectors for plants and animals

- Pathogens have learnt to transfer their genes into plants and animals – scientists **disarm** them and use them as vectors.
- ***Agrobacterium tumefaciens*** – a pathogen of **several dicot plants**.
  - Delivers a piece of DNA known as **T-DNA** to transform normal plant cells into a **tumour**, directing them to produce chemicals the pathogen needs.
  - Its **Ti plasmid** (tumour-inducing) is modified into a cloning vector which is **no longer pathogenic** but can still deliver genes of interest into plants.
- **Retroviruses** in animals transform normal cells into **cancerous cells**. **Disarmed retroviruses** are now used to deliver desirable genes into animal cells.

### 3.4 Competent host (for transformation with recombinant DNA)

- DNA is a **hydrophilic molecule** – it **cannot pass through cell membranes**.
- Bacterial cells are made **"competent"** to take up DNA:
  1. Treat with a specific concentration of a **divalent cation such as calcium** → increases efficiency of DNA entry through **pores in the cell wall**.
  2. Incubate cells with recombinant DNA **on ice**.
  3. Give a brief **heat shock at 42°C**.
  4. Put back **on ice**.
- This enables bacteria to take up the recombinant DNA.

#### Other methods of introducing alien DNA

| Method | Used in | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| **Micro-injection** | **Animal cell** | Recombinant DNA **directly injected into the nucleus** |
| **Biolistics / gene gun** | **Plants** | Cells bombarded with **high-velocity micro-particles of gold or tungsten coated with DNA** |
| **Disarmed pathogen vectors** | Plants/animals | Allowed to infect the cell, they **transfer the recombinant DNA** into the host |

## 4. Processes of Recombinant DNA Technology

Sequence of steps (frequently asked as "arrange in correct order"):

1. Isolation of DNA
2. Fragmentation of DNA by restriction endonucleases
3. Isolation of a desired DNA fragment
4. Ligation of the DNA fragment into a vector
5. Transfer of the recombinant DNA into the host
6. Culturing the host cells in a medium at large scale and extraction of the desired product

### 4.1 Isolation of the genetic material (DNA)

- DNA is enclosed within membranes; it must be released in **pure form, free from other macromolecules**.
- Cells are broken open with enzymes:

| Source cell | Enzyme used to break the cell (wall) |
|---|---|
| **Bacteria** | **Lysozyme** |
| **Plant cells** | **Cellulase** |
| **Fungus** | **Chitinase** |

- Genes are located on long DNA molecules **intertwined with proteins such as histones**.
- **RNA** is removed by treatment with **ribonuclease**; **proteins** removed with **protease**.
- Other molecules removed by appropriate treatments.
- Purified DNA **precipitates** out after addition of **chilled ethanol**.
- Seen as a **collection of fine threads in suspension** – can be removed by **spooling**.

> **Exam Tip**
> "Chilled ethanol" precipitates DNA. Ribonuclease removes RNA, protease removes proteins – a classic match-the-column.

### 4.2 Cutting of DNA at specific locations

- Purified DNA is incubated with the restriction enzyme under **optimal conditions**.
- **Agarose gel electrophoresis** is used to check the progress of restriction digestion.
- Source DNA and vector DNA are cut with the **same restriction enzyme**.
- Cut-out "gene of interest" from source DNA and cut vector with space are **mixed** and **ligase** is added → **recombinant DNA**.

### 4.3 Amplification of gene of interest using PCR

- **PCR – Polymerase Chain Reaction** – multiple copies of the gene (or DNA) of interest are synthesised ***in vitro***.
- Requirements:
  - **Two sets of primers** – small chemically synthesised **oligonucleotides complementary to regions of DNA**
  - **Template DNA**
  - **Deoxynucleotides**
  - **Thermostable DNA polymerase**

| Step | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Denaturation** | Double-stranded DNA **separated** by **high temperature** |
| **2. Annealing** | **Primers bind** to complementary regions of single-stranded DNA |
| **3. Extension** | DNA polymerase **extends the primers** using nucleotides and genomic DNA as template |

- Repeated many times → DNA segment amplified to approximately **one billion times (1 billion copies)**.
- Enzyme: **thermostable DNA polymerase isolated from *Thermus aquaticus*** (Taq polymerase) – **remains active during the high temperature** used for denaturation.
- Amplified fragment can be ligated with a vector for further cloning.

> **PYQ Trend**
> PCR has been asked repeatedly: correct order of steps (denaturation → annealing → extension), source of Taq polymerase (*Thermus aquaticus*), role of primers, and its use in **early diagnosis** (see Biotechnology and Its Applications).

### 4.4 Insertion of recombinant DNA into the host cell/organism

- Recipient cells are made **competent** and take up DNA.
- If recombinant DNA bearing an antibiotic-resistance gene (e.g., **ampicillin**) is transferred into *E. coli*, the host becomes **transformed into ampicillin-resistant cells**.
- Spreading transformed cells on agar plates containing ampicillin → **only transformants grow**; untransformed cells die.
- Here the ampicillin resistance gene is a **selectable marker**.

### 4.5 Obtaining the foreign gene product

- Ultimate aim is to produce a **desirable protein**.
- If a protein-encoding gene is expressed in a **heterologous host**, it is called a **recombinant protein**.
- Cells harbouring cloned genes can be grown **on a small scale in the laboratory** or in large volumes.
- **Continuous culture system** – used medium is **drained out from one side** while **fresh medium is added from the other** → cells kept in their **physiologically most active log/exponential phase** → **larger biomass and higher yields** of the desired protein.

#### Bioreactors

- Large-scale production needs **bioreactors** – **large volumes (100–1000 litres)** of culture can be processed.
- A bioreactor provides **optimal conditions** for obtaining the desired product: **temperature, pH, substrate, salts, vitamins, oxygen**.
- Most commonly used: **stirring type (stirred-tank) bioreactor**.

| Type | Features |
|---|---|
| **Simple stirred-tank bioreactor** | Usually **cylindrical or with a curved base** to facilitate mixing; **stirrer** facilitates **even mixing and oxygen availability** throughout the bioreactor |
| **Sparged stirred-tank bioreactor** | **Sterile air bubbles are sparged** (bubbled) through the medium → **increased surface area for oxygen transfer** |

- Parts/systems of a bioreactor:
  - **Agitator system**
  - **Oxygen delivery system**
  - **Foam control system**
  - **Temperature control system**
  - **pH control system**
  - **Sampling ports** – to withdraw small volumes of culture periodically

#### Downstream processing

- Processes after biosynthesis, before the product is ready for marketing:
  - **Separation and purification** – collectively called **downstream processing**.
  - Product is formulated with suitable **preservatives**.
  - For drugs – **clinical trials** required.
  - **Strict quality control testing** required for each product.
- Downstream processing and quality control **vary from product to product**.
